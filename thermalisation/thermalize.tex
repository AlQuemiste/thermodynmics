\title{Thermalization of Subsystems:\\
Coupled Oscillators, Heat Baths, and Internal Baths}
\author{}
\date{28 September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A closed Hamiltonian system never thermalizes as a whole: its fine-grained entropy, and its distance to any Gibbs state, are constants of the motion.
Thermalization is a property of reduced descriptions, and this note examines under which conditions a reduced description thermalizes, using four systems of increasing complexity.
(i) A closed chain of harmonic oscillators is solved exactly; its normal-mode energies are conserved, so it does not thermalize, not even in its reduced observables.
(ii) The same kind of system, weakly coupled to a large classical harmonic bath prepared at temperature $ T $, is solved by exact elimination of the bath followed by second-order averaging; each normal mode relaxes to $ T $ at a golden-rule rate and the system distribution becomes the Gibbs distribution, while the system and bath together remain non-thermal.
(iii) For a quantum system weakly coupled to a bosonic bath, the decoherence factor, the Debye--Waller factor, the relaxation rates and the stationary Gibbs state follow from two properties of the bath correlation function; the temperature enters only as the Kubo--Martin--Schwinger parameter of the bath.
(iv) For the isolated Bose gas of Lenk \textit{et al.}\ [Entropy \textbf{27}, 636 (2025)], the Mori--Zwanzig projection formalism, evaluated by exact diagonalization, shows that the observed level occupations are driven by an internal bath formed by the rest of the closed system and reach values within a few per cent of thermal ones. A finite-size memory remains, which in equilibrium scales as $ D^{-1/2} $ with the Hilbert-space dimension $ D $. With slow and fast variables defined by the gap in the relaxation spectrum of the Mori propagator, the energy is the only slow variable: the effective coupling to the internal bath is not weak, and the relaxation is non-Markovian. The stationary entanglement entropy reported by Lenk \textit{et al.}\ is reproduced; their interpretation is examined critically.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Microscopic dynamics is reversible and conserves phase-space volume, while macroscopic systems relax irreversibly to thermal equilibrium.
This note works out, on explicitly solvable or exactly computable examples, how the two statements are reconciled.
The answer is organized around one distinction: the state of a closed system as a whole versus a reduced description of it, such as the state of a subsystem or the values of a few observables.

Section~\ref{sec:foundations} sets out the general frame: the part of the Hamiltonian that no description contains is a stochastic operator, known only through a prior over its coefficients, and the entropy and the second law are traced to it.
The first statement is exact and general: a closed system does not thermalize as a whole (Sec.~\ref{sec:defs}).
The question addressed in the rest of the note is therefore when a reduced description thermalizes, and what supplies the necessary irreversibility.
Four systems are treated in turn.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=2.2em]
\item A closed chain of coupled harmonic oscillators (Sec.~\ref{sec:chain}). The exact normal-mode solution shows that it cannot thermalize by itself, because every normal-mode energy is conserved.
\item A system of coupled harmonic oscillators weakly coupled to a much larger harmonic bath (Sec.~\ref{sec:bath}). The bath is eliminated exactly and the remaining equations are solved perturbatively in the coupling. The system thermalizes at the bath temperature; the system and bath together do not.
\item A quantum system weakly coupled to a large bosonic bath (Sec.~\ref{sec:quantum}). Decoherence, temperature and entropy of the system are derived from the correlation function of the bath.
\item An isolated, interacting Bose gas in five single-particle levels, the model of Lenk \textit{et al.}~\cite{Lenk2025} (Sec.~\ref{sec:mz}). No external bath exists. The Mori--Zwanzig projection formalism, introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec:mz-method}, splits the dynamics exactly into the observed variables and the remaining ones, which act as an internal bath. The section defines slow and fast variables precisely and tests whether the observed variables are slow.
\end{enumerate}
Each section states the method used and why it applies.
Natural units, $ = k_{\mathrm{B}} = c = 1 $, are used throughout: energies, frequencies and temperatures are measured in the same unit, and the entropy is a pure number.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} summarizes the four cases in Table~\ref{tab:summary} and lists open questions.

\section{Microscopic foundations: heat, information and the second law}
\label{sec:foundations}

Equilibrium statistical mechanics takes the ensembles as given.
This section asks where they come from, and sets the frame for the worked examples of the later sections.
The starting point is a negative result.
The microscopic dynamics of a closed system --- a density matrix $ \rho $ evolving under a Hamiltonian by the von~Neumann equation --- is reversible, and it conserves the Gibbs entropy $ S[\rho] = -\Tr(\rho\ln\rho) $ exactly.
The entropy therefore cannot be obtained from the microscopic equations of motion, however they are manipulated.

What enters instead is the part of the Hamiltonian that no description contains.
The Hamiltonian of a real body is
\[
  \hat H_{\rm tot} = \hat H + \hat H' ,
\]
where $ \hat H $ is the model, the part that is known and treated microscopically, and $ \hat H' $ comprises everything else: the degrees of freedom of the body itself that the model does not resolve --- strongly interacting states on energy and length scales beyond its ultraviolet or infrared cutoffs --- and the couplings to the walls, to the radiation field, to the molecules of a surrounding fluid, and to the contacts through which particles enter and leave.
The effects of $ \hat H' $ are not small, but $ \hat H' $ cannot be written down in detail, either because it is too complex or because its mechanism is simply not known.
The ideal gas is the simplest instance: its model Hamiltonian $ \sum_ip_i^{2}/2m $ contains no collisions, yet without collisions the gas would never equilibrate.
Boltzmann's kinetic theory restores them through the \emph{Stosszahlansatz}, the assumption that the velocities of two molecules about to collide are uncorrelated; this single assumption, not the mechanics of the collisions, is what produces the $ H $-theorem and the approach to the Maxwell distribution.
Equilibrium statistical mechanics goes one step further and keeps nothing of the collisions at all: their only trace is that the gas has a temperature and an entropy.
A Brownian particle is the other paradigm: it is kicked by the molecules of the fluid, and nothing about the individual kicks is known.

Formally, $ \hat H' $ is an operator of the same kind as $ \hat H $ and can be expanded in the same operator basis.
The difference is that its coefficients are not known beforehand, and what is known about them is a probability distribution, a prior in the Bayesian sense (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_second}).
The randomness that this introduces is not quantum mechanical.
It is stochastic, an expression of the lack of knowledge of the full Hamiltonian, and it adds to the von~Neumann equation of the known part a dissipative term that the equation by itself cannot produce.
The long-time evolution under the full Hamiltonian, once the relaxation processes driven by this term have run their course, ends in a steady state of higher entropy, described by an ensemble; a two-level system (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_tls}) shows every step, and particles in an unknown potential (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_anderson}) show what is required of $ \hat H' $ beyond its being unknown.
Statistical mechanics does not attempt to describe $ \hat H' $.
It replaces it by \emph{devices} with very few parameters.
The entropy, with its conjugate $ T $, is the device for the unknown exchange of energy; the term $ -\mu\hat N $ added to the Hamiltonian is the device for the unknown mechanism of creation/annihilation or exchange of particles.

\subsection{Heat and work}
\label{sec:found_heat}

Heat and work are not two terms into which $ \dd U $ can be split by inspecting the state of the system; they are distinguished by the \emph{channel} through which the energy arrives.
Let the Hamiltonian $ \hat H(\lambda) $ of the system depend on parameters $ \lambda $ that the experimenter controls (volume, field, strain), let the system be coupled, if at all, to a bath with Hamiltonian $ \hat H_{\rm B} $, and let $ U = \Tr(\rho\hat H) $ with $ \rho $ the state of the system.
System and bath together are isolated except for the agent that moves $ \lambda $: their joint state evolves unitarily under $ \hat H_{\rm tot} = \hat H(\lambda(t)) + \hat H_{\rm B} + \hat H_{\rm int} $, whose only time dependence is through $ \lambda(t) $.
Their energy therefore changes at the rate
\[
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Tr\big(\rho_{\rm tot}\hat H_{\rm tot}\big)
  = \Tr\big(\rho_{\rm tot}\,\partial_t\hat H_{\rm tot}\big)
  = \sum_j\Big\langle\frac{\partial\hat H}{\partial\lambda_j}\Big\rangle\dot\lambda_j ,
\]
the term $ \Tr(\hat H_{\rm tot}\,\dot\rho_{\rm tot}) = -\ii\Tr\big(\hat H_{\rm tot}[\hat H_{\rm tot}, \rho_{\rm tot}]\big) $ vanishing by cyclicity of the trace.
This is the \emph{work}, the energy delivered by the agent, $ \dbar W = \sum_j\langle\partial\hat H/\partial\lambda_j\rangle\,\dd\lambda_j $; for $ \lambda = V $ in equilibrium, $ \langle\partial\hat H/\partial V\rangle = -P $ and $ \dbar W = -P\,\dd V $.
The \emph{heat} is the energy the system receives from the bath, $ \dbar Q = -\dd\langle\hat H_{\rm B}\rangle $ when the coupling energy $ \langle\hat H_{\rm int}\rangle $ is negligible (weak coupling), and the first law $ \dd U = \dbar W + \dbar Q $ is the conservation of the total energy.
Without a bath there is no heat at all: a thermally isolated system receives energy only as work.
In the language of this section, work is the energy that arrives through the known part of the Hamiltonian, the controlled parameters $ \lambda $, and heat is the energy that arrives through the part that is not known in detail, $ \hat H' = \hat H_{\rm B} + \hat H_{\rm int} $, especially, $ \hat H_{\rm int} $.

Neither $ \dbar W $ nor $ \dbar Q $ is the differential of a function of the state.
Along a quasi-static sequence of Gibbs states,\footnote{\label{fn:gibbsstate}\textbf{Gibbs state.}
A density matrix of the form $ \rho = e^{-\beta\hat K}/\Tr e^{-\beta\hat K} $ for some Hermitian $ \hat K $: the canonical state for $ \hat K = \hat H $, the grand canonical state for $ \hat K = \hat H - \mu\hat N $, and so on.
It is the equilibrium state of every statistical ensemble.} however, the heat is $ T\,\dd S $.
With occupations $ p_a = e^{-\beta E_a}/Z $ of the levels $ E_a(\lambda) $, the energy changes by $ \dd U = \sum_a(p_a\,\dd E_a + E_a\,\dd p_a) $, of which the first term is the work and the second the heat, and since $ \sum_a \dd p_a = 0 $,
\[
  \dd S = -\sum_a \dd p_a\ln p_a = \beta\sum_aE_a\,\dd p_a = \frac{\dbar Q}{T} .
\]

\subsection{Reversibility of the microscopic dynamics}
\label{sec:found_unitary}

Consider an isolated system, $ \ii\partial_t\rho = [\hat H, \rho] $, so that $ \rho(t) = \hat U\rho(0)\hat U^\dagger $ with $ \hat U = e^{-\ii \hat Ht} $.
The evolution is reversible in two senses.
It is \emph{invertible}, $ \rho(0) = \hat U^\dagger\rho(t)\,\hat U $: the state at any time determines the state at every other time, so no information about the initial state is ever lost.
And if $ \hat H $ commutes with the antiunitary time-reversal operator $ \Theta $, then with $ \rho(t) $ the time-reversed history $ \Theta\rho(-t)\Theta^{-1} $ is also a solution, so that every process can, in principle, run backwards (Loschmidt's objection to Boltzmann, 1876).
Two exact statements follow.

\emph{The Gibbs entropy is constant.}
$ \rho(t) $ and $ \rho(0) $ are unitarily similar, so they have the same eigenvalues, and $ S[\rho(t)] = S[\rho(0)] $; more generally $ \Tr f(\rho) $ is conserved for every function $ f $.
The Gibbs entropy of an isolated system never increases.

\emph{The energy distribution is constant.}
Let $ P_t(E) = \Tr[\rho(t)\,\delta(\hat H - E)] $.
Since $ \hat U $ commutes with every function of $ \hat H $, cyclicity of the trace gives
\[
  P_t(E) = \Tr\big[\rho(0)\,\hat U^\dagger\delta(\hat H - E)\,\hat U\big]
  = \Tr\big[\rho(0)\,\delta(\hat H - E)\big] = P_0(E) .
\]
All moments of $ \hat H $ are constants of motion.

Both statements hold for \emph{any} Hamiltonian, and in particular for the full $ \hat H_{\rm tot} $ of system and surroundings.
No functional of $ \rho $ determined by a completely known microscopic dynamics can therefore grow monotonically: a system described exactly has no second law.
The macroscopic irreversibility (e.g., a gas filling a vessel, heat flowing from hot to cold, a stirred fluid warming up) must come from what the description leaves out, i.e. from $ \hat H' $.

\subsection{The unknown part of the Hamiltonian}
\label{sec:found_second}

\paragraph{No body is isolated.}
The many-body levels of a macroscopic body are spaced by about $ e^{-S} $ in natural units, i.e.\ by a quantity of order $ e^{-N} $.
Any coupling to the outside (e.g., a thermal photon scattered by the walls, the vibrations of the container, the cosmic radiation, the gravitational field of distant masses) has matrix elements between these levels that exceed their spacing by a factor exponential in $ N $.
The estimate is a sum rule.
Let $ \hat H' $ be a coupling of energy scale $ \epsilon $, as small as one likes but independent of $ N $, and let $ |a\rangle $ be an eigenstate of $ \hat H $.
The row sum $ \sum_b|H'_{ab}|^{2} = \langle a|\hat H'^{2}|a\rangle \sim \epsilon^{2} $ is shared among the states $ b $ that $ \hat H' $ connects to $ a $, of which there are of order $ e^{S} $ within the energy range that a local coupling can reach, so a typical matrix element is $ |H'_{ab}| \sim \epsilon\,e^{-S/2} $, while the level spacing is $ \sim \epsilon_0 e^{-S} $ with $ \epsilon_0 $ a microscopic energy.
Their ratio, $ (\epsilon/\epsilon_0)\,e^{S/2} $, is astronomically large for any $ \epsilon $ that is not itself exponentially small in $ N $.
Such a coupling mixes the eigenstates of the model Hamiltonian $ \hat H $ over an energy range that contains astronomically many of them, and the resulting state depends on details of $ \hat H' $ that are neither known nor controlled.
The eigenstates of an ``isolated'' macroscopic body are therefore an idealization: they are sharp only on a scale that the real body never respects.

The sensitivity is amplified by chaos.
Classically, nearby trajectories of a chaotic system separate exponentially, so that an initially negligible perturbation soon dominates; Berry (1978) estimated that neglecting the gravitational pull of a single electron at the edge of the observable universe makes the trajectory of a molecule in a gas unpredictable after about fifty collisions.
The quantum counterpart is the \emph{Loschmidt echo}
\[
  M(t) = \big|\langle\psi|\,e^{\ii \hat Ht}\,e^{-\ii (\hat H + \hat H')t}\,|\psi\rangle\big|^{2} ,
\]
the fidelity with which a state evolved forward under the true Hamiltonian $ \hat H + \hat H' $ is returned to its start by reversing only the model Hamiltonian $ \hat H $.
For systems whose classical limit is chaotic, $ M(t) $ decays exponentially, and in a broad range of perturbation strengths at a rate set by the Lyapunov exponent of the unperturbed dynamics and independent of $ \hat H' $ (Jalabert and Pastawski, 2001).
A coupling too weak to be measured thus suffices to make the microscopic history unrepeatable and irreversible in practice.

\paragraph{Brownian motion: the random kicks as a device.}
A colloidal particle of mass $ m $ moves in an external potential $ \mathcal{V}(x) $ inside a fluid.
The known Hamiltonian is $ p^{2}/2m + \mathcal{V}(x) $; the unknown part is the interaction with some $ 10^{20} $ fluid molecules, whose positions and velocities nobody knows.
Langevin's equation (1908) replaces that part by two terms,
\[
  m\ddot x = -\mathcal{V}'(x) - \gamma\dot x + \zeta(t) , \qquad
  \langle\zeta(t)\rangle = 0 , \qquad
  \langle\zeta(t)\,\zeta(t')\rangle = 2\gamma T\,\delta(t - t') :
\]
a friction $ -\gamma\dot x $, the average effect of the kicks, and a random force $ \zeta(t) $, their fluctuation.
The two are not independent.
The strength of the noise must be $ 2\gamma T $ for a fluid at temperature $ T $ to bring the kinetic energy of the particle to its equipartition value $ T/2 $; this is the fluctuation--dissipation relation, in Einstein's form (1905) $ D = T/\gamma $ for the diffusion constant.
The whole Hamiltonian of the fluid and of its coupling to the particle has been replaced by two numbers, $ \gamma $ and $ T $.

On time scales long compared with $ m/\gamma $ the inertia can be dropped, $ \gamma\dot x = -\mathcal{V}'(x) + \zeta(t) $, and the probability density $ P(x, t) $ of the particle obeys the Smoluchowski equation
\[
  \frac{\partial P}{\partial t}
  = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigg[\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(\mathcal{V}'P + T\,\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\Big)\bigg]
  = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigg[\frac{P}{\gamma}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(\mathcal{V} + T\ln P\big)\bigg] ,
\]
the drift coming from the force and the diffusion from the noise.
Define
\[
  F[P] := \int \dd x\;P\,\big(\mathcal{V} + T\ln P\big)
  = \langle\mathcal{V}\rangle - T\,S[P] , \qquad
  S[P] = -\int \dd x\;P\ln P .
\]
With $ \psi := \mathcal{V} + T\ln P $, the functional derivative is $ \delta F/\delta P = \psi + T $, and an integration by parts (the boundary terms vanish for a confining potential) gives
\[
  \frac{\dd F}{\dd t} = \int \dd x\;\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}\,(\psi + T)
  = -\int \dd x\;\frac{P}{\gamma}\Big(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\Big)^{2} \;\leq\; 0 ,
\]
with equality only if $ \psi $ is constant, i.e.\ for $ P \propto e^{-\mathcal{V}/T} $.
(With inertia kept, the same holds for the density in $ (x, p) $, with $ \langle p^{2}/2m + \mathcal{V}\rangle $ in place of $ \langle\mathcal{V}\rangle $.)

This calculation shows exactly where the entropy comes from.
The known force alone, $ T \to 0 $, would minimize $ \langle\mathcal{V}\rangle $ and send the particle to the bottom of the well.
The noise alone would spread it without limit.
The term $ -T\,S[P] $ in the functional that the motion minimizes is the entire imprint of the ``unknown kicks'': it vanishes with them.
The friction $ \gamma $ fixes how fast the minimum is reached, not where it lies, and the equilibrium state retains $ T $ alone.
The entropy enters not from the known dynamics but as the representation of the unknown part.

\paragraph{The unknown part need not be external.}
Nothing in the device requires the kicks to come from outside the body, nor any external time-dependent drive.
Every model resolves the degrees of freedom of a body only within a window of length and energy scales.
Beyond its ultraviolet cutoff (short lengths, high energies) and its infrared cutoff (long lengths, low energies) lie states that could be strongly interacting and far too complex to be treated microscopically, and they are related to $ \hat H' $.
Their effect on the retained degrees of freedom is modeled, crudely, by a stochastic force or potential, as the fluid was for the Brownian particle.
The conduction electrons of a metal damp a local magnetic moment and make its orientation fluctuate (Korringa relaxation, Gilbert damping); the lattice is the bath of the nuclear spins (the spin--lattice relaxation time $ T_1 $) and the phonons are a bath for the conduction electrons.\footnote{\label{fn:morizwanzig}\textbf{Mori--Zwanzig projection.}
The exact form of this elimination is the projection-operator formalism of Zwanzig and Mori~\cite{Zwanzig1960,Mori1965} (Sec.~\ref{sec:mz-method}).
Projecting the equations of motion onto a chosen set of slow variables gives for them a \emph{generalized Langevin equation}: a systematic (mean-field) force, a friction term with a memory kernel built from the correlations of the eliminated fast variables, and a residual force that is orthogonal to the slow variables and is treated as noise.
The memory kernel and the noise correlation are related by a fluctuation--dissipation relation at the temperature of the bath, as $ \gamma $ and $ 2\gamma T $ are in Langevin's equation.
Nothing is approximated in the derivation; the approximation enters when the memory kernel is replaced by a constant friction (Markov limit) and the orthogonal force by white noise.}
The short-wavelength fluctuations of an order parameter $ \phi $, once eliminated, leave on the long-wavelength ones a damping and a noise,
\[
  \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} = -\Gamma\,\frac{\delta F[\phi]}{\delta\phi} + \zeta ,
  \qquad
  \langle\zeta(\mathbf{r}, t)\,\zeta(\mathbf{r}', t')\rangle
  = 2\Gamma T\,\delta(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}')\,\delta(t - t') ,
\]
the field-theoretic Langevin equation (model~A of Hohenberg and Halperin, 1977), whose stationary distribution is $ \propto e^{-F[\phi]/T} $ by the same calculation as above.
In this example the ``known Hamiltonian'' $ F[\phi] $ of the retained modes is itself a coarse-grained free energy, of Landau--Ginzburg form, which already contains the entropy of the eliminated modes: the device is applied scale by scale.
In all these cases the total Hamiltonian may be time-independent and the body isolated; seen from the retained variables, the eliminated ones act as a random force all the same.
Isolation from the outside therefore does not exempt a macroscopic body from the second law: its own unresolved degrees of freedom suffice.

\paragraph{Unknown coefficients in a known basis.}
The same mechanism can be stated for a quantum system without any model of the noise, and precisely.
Choose a basis of Hermitian operators $ \hat\Lambda_\alpha $ on the Hilbert space of the body, orthonormal under the trace, $ \Tr(\hat\Lambda_\alpha\hat\Lambda_\beta) = \delta_{\alpha\beta} $: for a spin $ 1/2 $ the unit matrix and the three Pauli matrices, each divided by $ \sqrt2 $; for $ N $ spins their products; for particles, the normalized products of creation and annihilation operators.
Every Hermitian operator has a unique expansion in this basis with real coefficients, and so have both parts of the Hamiltonian,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hprime_expansion}
  \hat H = \sum_\alpha h_\alpha\,\hat\Lambda_\alpha , \qquad
  \hat H' = \sum_\alpha\eta_\alpha\,\hat\Lambda_\alpha , \qquad
  h_\alpha = \Tr(\hat\Lambda_\alpha\hat H) , \quad \eta_\alpha = \Tr(\hat\Lambda_\alpha\hat H') .
\end{equation}
The two parts are operators of the same kind.
The only difference is that the $ h_\alpha $ are known numbers and the $ \eta_\alpha $ are not.
Nothing in $ \hat H' $ is random: in a given body the $ \eta_\alpha $ have definite values.
What is missing is the knowledge of these values, and a state of incomplete knowledge is described by a probability density $ \pi(\eta) $ on the space of the coefficients $ \eta = (\eta_1, \eta_2, \dots) $, a \emph{prior} in the Bayesian sense.
The prior is not a property of the body.
It summarizes what is known about $ \hat H' $ before the experiment, and it changes when more is learned.

For each value of $ \eta $ the evolution is unitary, generated by $ \hat H + \hat H'_\eta $ with $ \hat H'_\eta = \sum_\alpha\eta_\alpha\hat\Lambda_\alpha $: $ \rho_\eta(t) = \hat U_\eta(t)\,\rho(0)\,\hat U_\eta^\dagger(t) $.
The prediction for an observable $ \hat O $ weights the prediction of each realization with the prior,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prior_average}
  \int\!\dd\eta\;\pi(\eta)\,\Tr\big[\rho_\eta(t)\,\hat O\big] = \Tr\big[\varrho(t)\,\hat O\big] ,
  \qquad
  \varrho(t) := \int\!\dd\eta\;\pi(\eta)\;\hat U_\eta(t)\,\rho(0)\,\hat U_\eta^\dagger(t) .
\end{equation}
Because expectation values are linear in the density matrix, $ \varrho(t) $ contains every prediction that can be made.
It is the \emph{description} of the body, as distinct from the state $ \rho_\eta(t) $ that the body would be assigned if $ \hat H' $ were known; in the paragraphs that follow, whenever $ \hat H' $ is involved, the density matrix meant is $ \varrho $.
Two probabilities of different origin are merged in it.
The first is quantum mechanical, the Born rule contained in each $ \rho_\eta $, and it would be present with $ \hat H' $ known exactly.
The second is stochastic, the prior $ \pi(\eta) $, an ordinary probability density and not an amplitude, and it expresses nothing but the lack of knowledge of the full Hamiltonian.
The operator $ \hat H' $ itself is as quantum mechanical as $ \hat H $: its matrix elements $ H'_{ab} = \sum_\alpha\eta_\alpha\langle a|\hat\Lambda_\alpha|b\rangle $ are complex, and their phases produce interference in every realization.
Only their values are uncertain.
No measurement on the body alone separates the two.

Each $ \hat U_\eta $ is reversible and conserves the entropy, but the entropy of the description cannot be lower than that of the initial state.
The relative entropy $ D(\rho \Vert \sigma) := \Tr[\rho(\ln\rho - \ln\sigma)] $ of two density matrices is non-negative and vanishes only for $ \rho = \sigma $ (Klein's inequality; Sec.~\ref{sec:invariants}).
Applying it to each $ \rho_\eta $ against $ \varrho $ (the support of $ \rho_\eta $ lies in that of $ \varrho $ for every $ \eta $ of non-zero weight) and using $ \int \dd\eta\,\pi\,\rho_\eta = \varrho $,
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  0 \;\leq\; \int\!\dd\eta\;\pi(\eta)\,D(\rho_\eta \Vert \varrho)
  &= \int\!\dd\eta\;\pi(\eta)\Tr(\rho_\eta\ln\rho_\eta) - \Tr(\varrho\ln\varrho) \\
  &= \Big(S[\varrho] - \int\!\dd\eta\;\pi(\eta)\,S[\rho_\eta]\Big) ,
  \end{aligned}
\]
and $ S[\rho_\eta(t)] = S[\rho(0)] $ for every $ \eta $, so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prior_entropy}
  S[\varrho(t)] \;\geq\; S[\rho(0)] \qquad\text{at every time } t ,
\end{equation}
with equality only if all the $ \rho_\eta(t) $ coincide, i.e.\ only if all the values of the coefficients that cannot be excluded lead to the same state.
The functional $ S $ is the one that the von~Neumann equation conserves; what is new is its argument.
The equation of motion moves each $ \rho_\eta $ and leaves its entropy alone, and the entropy of $ \varrho $ grows by the uncertainty about $ \eta $ that the evolution has imprinted on the state.
For a prior concentrated on discrete values $ \eta_k $ with weights $ q_k $ the growth is bounded by that uncertainty,
\[
  S[\varrho(t)] - S[\rho(0)] \;\leq\; -\sum_kq_k\ln q_k ,
\]
the Shannon entropy of the prior: the missing information about the Hamiltonian is converted, at most in full, into missing information about the state.
This is the point at which an entropy of the Gibbs--Shannon kind enters beside the dynamics.
It is not produced by the von~Neumann equation, and it is not quantum mechanical in origin.

\paragraph{The prior.}
Whatever is known about $ \hat H' $ restricts $ \pi $.
A non-zero mean $ \overline{\eta_\alpha} $ (the overline denotes the average over the prior) is a piece of knowledge and belongs to $ h_\alpha $, so the prior is centerd at $ \eta = 0 $.
If $ \hat H' $ is known to conserve a quantity $ \hat X $ (the particle number of a body with impermeable walls, the energy of an insulated one to within a macroscopically narrow shell), only the basis operators that commute with $ \hat X $ are \emph{admitted}: the coefficients of all the others vanish with certainty.
And the strength of $ \hat H' $ is known in order of magnitude.
Beyond this nothing is known, and the prior is \emph{flat}: no direction in the space of the admitted coefficients is preferred to any other, so that $ \pi $ depends on $ \eta $ only through $ \sum_\alpha\eta_\alpha^{2} = \Tr\hat H'^{2} $ and is invariant under every admitted change of basis $ \hat H' \to \hat V\hat H'\hat V^\dagger $.
A density that is constant over the whole space of coefficients cannot be normalized, and would describe a perturbation of unbounded strength; the flat prior of a given strength, the density of largest Shannon entropy at fixed $ \overline{\Tr\hat H'^{2}} $, is the Gaussian
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flat_prior}
  \pi(\eta) \;\propto\; \exp\Big(-\sum_\alpha\frac{\eta_\alpha^{2}}{2v^{2}}\Big)
  = \exp\Big(-\frac{\Tr\hat H'^{2}}{2v^{2}}\Big) ,
\end{equation}
with independent coefficients of equal variance $ v^{2} $.
This is Wigner's Gaussian ensemble of random matrices, introduced in the 1950s for the same purpose: the Hamiltonian of a heavy nucleus, too complex to be known.
On a space of $ W $ states it gives $ \overline{|H'_{ab}|^{2}} = v^{2} $ for every matrix element and hence $ \overline{\langle a|\hat H'^{2}|a\rangle} = Wv^{2} $, so that the coupling of energy scale $ \epsilon $ estimated above has $ v = \epsilon/\sqrt W = \epsilon\,e^{-S/2} $.

\paragraph{Static coefficients: a steady state.}
In an isolated body whose total Hamiltonian does not depend on time the $ \eta_\alpha $ are constants.
Let $ E_n(\eta) $ and $ \hat\Pi_n^{\eta} $ be the eigenvalues and eigenprojectors of $ \hat H + \hat H'_\eta $.
Then
\[
  \varrho(t) = \int\!\dd\eta\;\pi(\eta)\sum_{m,n}
  e^{-\ii [E_m(\eta) - E_n(\eta)]t}\;\hat\Pi_m^{\eta}\,\rho(0)\,\hat\Pi_n^{\eta} .
\]
In the terms with $ m \neq n $ the phase varies with $ \eta $, and for a continuous prior their integral vanishes at long times (the Riemann--Lebesgue lemma), namely for $ t \gg 1/\delta E $ with $ \delta E $ the spread of the level differences under the prior.
What remains is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prior_steady}
  \varrho_\infty = \int\!\dd\eta\;\pi(\eta)\sum_n\hat\Pi_n^{\eta}\,\rho(0)\,\hat\Pi_n^{\eta} .
\end{equation}
After the transients the description is stationary, with an entropy higher than the initial one by Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior_entropy}, and this without an external agent and without any average over time; even the recurrences that a finite system shows for each single $ \eta $ are absent from $ \varrho(t) $.
Two reservations keep this from being the second law.
The steady state~\eqref{eq:prior_steady} in general remembers more of $ \rho(0) $ than the conserved quantities (the two-level system of Sec.~\ref{sec:found_tls} retains one third of its initial polarization, and particles in a static unknown potential may never leave the neighborhood of their initial positions, Sec.~\ref{sec:found_anderson}).
And the entropy gained can be taken back.
Nothing has happened to the body that a realization $ \hat U_\eta^\dagger $ would not undo; the information resides in the correlation between the state and the values of the coefficients, and static coefficients can be found out.
A measurement on the body updates the prior by Bayes' rule, and a coefficient determined in this way, which is what spectroscopy does, moves from $ \hat H' $ to $ \hat H $.
A static $ \hat H' $ can even be reversed without being learned, as in the spin echo discussed below.

\paragraph{Fluctuating coefficients: the dissipator.}
In general the unknown part is a \emph{stochastic operator}: a quantum operator like any other, expanded in the basis~\eqref{eq:Hprime_expansion}, whose coefficients are stochastic processes $ \eta_\alpha(t) $.
Static coefficients are the special case of an infinite correlation time.
The prior is then a distribution over the histories of the coefficients, and what is known of it is a strength $ v_\alpha $ and a correlation time $ \tau_c $.
When $ \tau_c $ is the shortest time in the problem, $ v_\alpha\tau_c \ll 1 $, the histories are Gaussian white noise,
\[
  \overline{\eta_\alpha(t)} = 0 , \qquad
  \overline{\eta_\alpha(t)\,\eta_\beta(t')} = \kappa_\alpha\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\delta(t - t') ,
  \qquad \kappa_\alpha = \int\!\dd t\;\overline{\eta_\alpha(t)\,\eta_\alpha(0)} \simeq 2v_\alpha^{2}\tau_c ,
\]
with one rate $ \kappa_\alpha \geq 0 $ for each basis operator, zero for those not admitted (a covariance that is not diagonal is made so by a rotation of the basis).
Over an interval $ \dd t $ a given history acts by the unitary $ \hat U = e^{-\ii \hat A} $ with $ \hat A = \hat H\,\dd t + \sum_\alpha\hat\Lambda_\alpha\,\dd w_\alpha $ and $ \dd w_\alpha = \int_t^{t + \dd t}\eta_\alpha(t')\,\dd t' $, so that
\[
  \hat U\rho_\eta\hat U^\dagger = \rho_\eta - \ii[\hat A, \rho_\eta]
  - \frac{1}{2}\big[\hat A, [\hat A, \rho_\eta]\big] + \dots
\]
The increments have $ \overline{\dd w_\alpha} = 0 $ and $ \overline{\dd w_\alpha\,\dd w_\beta} = \kappa_\alpha\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\dd t $, and they are independent of $ \rho_\eta(t) $, which depends on the earlier history only.
The average therefore factorizes, the second-order term contributes at order $ \dd t $, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dissipator}
  \frac{\dd\varrho}{\dd t} = -\ii[\hat H, \varrho]
  - \frac{1}{2}\sum_\alpha\kappa_\alpha\big[\hat\Lambda_\alpha, [\hat\Lambda_\alpha, \varrho]\big] ,
  \qquad
  -\frac{1}{2}\big[\hat\Lambda, [\hat\Lambda, \varrho]\big]
  = \hat\Lambda\varrho\hat\Lambda - \frac{1}{2}\big\{\hat\Lambda^{2}, \varrho\big\} .
\end{equation}
The first term is the von~Neumann equation of the known Hamiltonian.
The second is new: a \emph{dissipator}, of the form that Gorini, Kossakowski, Sudarshan and Lindblad (1976) found for the most general Markovian evolution of a density matrix, here with the Hermitian operators $ \sqrt{\kappa_\alpha}\,\hat\Lambda_\alpha $ as jump operators.
In the phenomenological use of the Lindblad equation the jump operators and their rates are postulated.
Here they are derived: the operators are the basis operators admitted in $ \hat H' $, and the rates are the widths of the prior.
No single realization obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipator}; each evolves unitarily, and the dissipator belongs to the description.
Four consequences follow.

\emph{The entropy grows monotonically.}
In the eigenbasis of the description, $ \varrho = \sum_np_n|n\rangle\langle n| $, the Hamiltonian term does not contribute to $ \dd S/\dd t = -\Tr(\dot\varrho\ln\varrho) $.
The dissipator does: with the identity $ \Tr([\hat\Lambda, \hat Y]\hat Z) = \Tr(\hat Y[\hat Z, \hat\Lambda]) $,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dissipator_entropy}
  \begin{aligned}
  \frac{\dd S}{\dd t} &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_\alpha\kappa_\alpha\Tr\big([\hat\Lambda_\alpha, \varrho]\,[\ln\varrho, \hat\Lambda_\alpha]\big) \\
  &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_\alpha\kappa_\alpha\sum_{m,n}\big|\langle m|\hat\Lambda_\alpha|n\rangle\big|^{2}\,
  (p_n - p_m)(\ln p_n - \ln p_m) \;\geq\; 0 ,
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
each term being non-negative because the logarithm is increasing.
Unlike Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior_entropy}, which compares the entropy with its initial value only, this is an increase at every instant, and it cannot be taken back: a coefficient that has fluctuated is no longer there to be learned or reversed.

\emph{The conserved quantities are those of the prior.}
For an observable $ \hat X $, Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipator} gives $ \dd\langle\hat X\rangle/\dd t = \ii\langle[\hat H, \hat X]\rangle - \tfrac12\sum_\alpha\kappa_\alpha\langle[\hat\Lambda_\alpha, [\hat\Lambda_\alpha, \hat X]]\rangle $, which vanishes in every state if $ \hat X $ commutes with $ \hat H $ and with all the admitted $ \hat\Lambda_\alpha $.
The whole distribution of such an $ \hat X $ is then constant.

\emph{The stationary state is a function of the conserved quantities alone.}
$ \dd S/\dd t = 0 $ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipator_entropy} requires $ p_m = p_n $ wherever a matrix element $ \langle m|\hat\Lambda_\alpha|n\rangle $ with $ \kappa_\alpha > 0 $ is non-zero, i.e.\ $ [\hat\Lambda_\alpha, \varrho_\infty] = 0 $ for every admitted operator, and stationarity then requires $ [\hat H, \varrho_\infty] = 0 $ as well.
The steady state commutes with everything that $ \hat H' $ may contain, and it is uniform over all the states that $ \hat H' $ can connect.

\emph{A flat prior gives the uniform distribution.}
Let all the Hermitian operators on a space of $ W $ states be admitted with the same rate $ \kappa $.
The completeness of the basis, $ \sum_\alpha\hat\Lambda_\alpha\hat Y\hat\Lambda_\alpha = \Tr(\hat Y)\,\mathbb{1} $ for every $ \hat Y $ and hence $ \sum_\alpha\hat\Lambda_\alpha^{2} = W\,\mathbb{1} $, reduces Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipator} to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flat_relax}
  \frac{\dd\varrho}{\dd t} = -\ii[\hat H, \varrho] - \kappa W\Big(\varrho - \frac{\mathbb{1}}{W}\Big) ,
  \qquad
  \varrho(t) = \frac{\mathbb{1}}{W}
  + e^{-\kappa Wt}\;\hat U(t)\Big(\rho(0) - \frac{\mathbb{1}}{W}\Big)\hat U^\dagger(t) ,
\end{equation}
with $ \hat U(t) = e^{-\ii \hat Ht} $: whatever the known Hamiltonian and whatever the initial state, the description relaxes exponentially to $ \mathbb{1}/W $, and $ S \to \ln W $.

For a body that exchanges nothing with its surroundings $ \hat H' $ conserves the energy to within a macroscopically small width: the admitted operators act within an energy shell of $ W $ states, $ \mathbb{1} $ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:flat_relax} is the projector onto the shell, and the steady state is the microcanonical one, with $ S = \ln W $.
The energy survives because $ \hat H' $ conserves it; everything else about the microstate is erased.
This is the second law for an isolated body, with its origin in view: the equal a priori probability of the microstates is the image, in the state, of the flat prior over the unknown part of the Hamiltonian.

One consequence must be stated.
A stochastic operator that does not commute with $ \hat H $ does work on the body.
The dissipator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipator} leaves $ \mathbb{1} $ invariant, and unless the admitted operators conserve the energy the steady state is uniform over all the states, whatever their energy: the body is heated without limit.
A steady state at a finite temperature is therefore tied to the conservation of the energy by $ \hat H' $, and it needs nothing else; no reservoir has to be introduced.
The uniform state on the shell of energy $ E $ has the entropy $ S(E) = \ln W(E) $ and hence a temperature, $ 1/T = \partial S/\partial E $, and every small part of the body is then in the canonical state at that temperature, because the number of states of the remainder of the body, when the part is in a state of energy $ E_a $, is $ W_{\rm rem}(E - E_a) \propto e^{-\beta E_a} $: the remainder takes the place of a reservoir.
The complementary route, in which the remainder is modeled explicitly as a Gaussian bosonic bath weakly coupled to the system and the temperature enters as the detailed-balance parameter of its correlation function, is worked out in Secs.~\ref{sec:quantum} and~\ref{sec:kms}.
The temperature is a property of the conserved energy and not a parameter of $ \hat H' $ or of its prior (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_tls}).

\paragraph{The master equation.}
The same mechanism takes a familiar form in the eigenbasis of the known Hamiltonian when $ \hat H' $ is weak.
Let $ |a\rangle $, $ E_a $ be the eigenstates of the model Hamiltonian $ \hat H $ and $ p_a = \langle a|\varrho|a\rangle $ their occupations.
Over a time $ \Delta t $, second-order perturbation theory in $ \hat H' $, averaged over the prior of its coefficients, gives Fermi's golden rule.
Under a flat prior the matrix elements $ H'_{ab} = \langle a|\hat H'|b\rangle $ of different transitions are independent and their phases uniformly distributed, so that the interference terms between different transitions average to zero and $ \varrho $ stays diagonal in $ |a\rangle $:
\[
  \frac{\dd p_a}{\dd t} = \sum_b W_{ab}\,(p_b - p_a) , \qquad
  W_{ab} = 2\pi\,\overline{|H'_{ab}|^{2}}\;\delta_{\Delta t}(E_a - E_b) ,
\]
with $ W_{ab} $ the rate from $ b $ to $ a $ and $ \delta_{\Delta t} $ a peak of width $ 1/\Delta t $.
Hermiticity of $ \hat H' $ makes the rates symmetric, $ W_{ab} = W_{ba} \geq 0 $.
The entropy $ S = -\sum_ap_a\ln p_a $ then obeys Boltzmann's $ H $-theorem in Pauli's form~\cite{Pauli1928}.
Since $ \sum_a\dot p_a = 0 $,
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \frac{\dd S}{\dd t} &= -\sum_a\dot p_a\ln p_a
  = -\sum_{a,b}W_{ab}\,(p_b - p_a)\ln p_a \\
  &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b}W_{ab}\,(p_b - p_a)(\ln p_b - \ln p_a) \;\geq\; 0 ,
  \end{aligned}
\]
the last form following by exchanging $ a \leftrightarrow b $ and averaging, with $ W_{ab} = W_{ba} $.
Each term is non-negative because the logarithm is increasing, and the sum vanishes only if $ p_a = p_b $ for every pair connected by a transition, i.e.\ for the uniform distribution on the energy shell.
This is the diagonal part of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipator}, whose matrix elements in a basis in which $ \varrho $ is diagonal give the same equation with $ W_{ab} = \sum_\alpha\kappa_\alpha|\langle a|\hat\Lambda_\alpha|b\rangle|^{2} $, and the inequality is Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipator_entropy}.
The irreversibility enters at one identifiable step: the phases of $ \rho $ and of $ \hat H' $ are discarded at every interval $ \Delta t $ (Pauli's repeated random-phase assumption).\footnote{\label{fn:pauli}\textbf{Pauli's random-phase assumption.}
In deriving the master equation (1928) Pauli assumed that at the start of every interval $ \Delta t $ the off-diagonal elements of $ \rho $ in the eigenbasis of $ \hat H $ have random phases, so that their contribution averages to zero and only the occupations $ p_a $ evolve; the assumption is \emph{repeated} at each step because unitary evolution would otherwise rebuild the phases.
It is the quantum form of Boltzmann's Stosszahlansatz.
The \emph{random-phase approximation} of many-body perturbation theory (Bohm and Pines, 1953; the summation of ring diagrams for the dielectric function) shares the name and the idea of discarding terms whose phases are uncorrelated, but it is an approximation to the equilibrium correlation functions of an interacting system and not a statement about irreversibility; the two should not be confused.}
Van~Hove~\cite{vanHove1955} showed that this step becomes exact for a macroscopic system in the weak-coupling limit, $ \hat H' \to 0 $ and $ t \to \infty $ with $ \|\hat H'\|^{2}t $ fixed.
With $ \hat H' $ known and the phases kept, the same calculation would give unitary evolution and a constant entropy.
The master equation is the evolution of the description, not of the microstate.

\paragraph{In contact with a bath.}
A bath is the extreme case of an unknown $ \hat H' $.
If each transition $ b \to a $ of the system takes the energy $ E_a - E_b $ from a large bath at temperature $ T $, the rates are no longer symmetric: the bath has fewer states at lower energy, by the factor $ \Omega_{\rm B}(E_{\rm tot} - E_a)/\Omega_{\rm B}(E_{\rm tot} - E_b) = e^{-\beta(E_a - E_b)} $, and the rates obey \emph{detailed balance}, $ W_{ab}\,p_b^{\rm eq} = W_{ba}\,p_a^{\rm eq} =: K_{ab} = K_{ba} $, with $ p_a^{\rm eq} = e^{-\beta E_a}/Z $.
The master equation becomes $ \dot p_a = \sum_b(W_{ab}p_b - W_{ba}p_a) = \sum_bK_{ab}(x_b - x_a) $ with $ x_a := p_a/p_a^{\rm eq} $, and the relative entropy $ D(p \Vert p^{\rm eq}) = \sum_ap_a\ln x_a $ obeys
\[
  \frac{\dd D}{\dd t} = \sum_a\dot p_a\ln x_a + \sum_a\dot p_a
  = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b}K_{ab}\,(x_b - x_a)(\ln x_b - \ln x_a) \;\leq\; 0 ,
\]
where the second sum vanished and the same exchange of $ a $ and $ b $ was used.
Since $ T\,D(p \Vert p^{\rm eq}) = F[p] - F $ with $ F[p] = U[p] - TS[p] $ (insert $ \ln p_a^{\rm eq} = -\beta(E_a - F) $, with $ F = -T\ln Z $), the free energy of the system decreases monotonically to its equilibrium value, as it did for the Brownian particle.
The bath enters only through the number $ T $ in the ratio of the rates; all other details of $ \hat H_{\rm B} $ and $ \hat H_{\rm int} $ are absorbed in the symmetric $ K_{ab} $, which, like $ \gamma $, sets how fast the system relaxes but not where.
The minimum principle of thermodynamics, that the free energy is minimal in equilibrium at a fixed temperature, appears here as the end point of the action of the unknown part of the Hamiltonian.
Section~\ref{sec:quantum} derives the detailed balance of the rates, and the Gibbs state that follows from it, for a system weakly coupled to a bosonic bath.

\paragraph{Dephasing is not loss.}
The model Hamiltonian $ \hat H $ by itself also makes the state look more disordered, but reversibly.
For non-degenerate energy differences the matrix elements in the eigenbasis are $ \langle a|\rho(t)|b\rangle = \rho_{ab}(0)\,e^{-\ii (E_a - E_b)t} $.
The off-diagonal elements oscillate, and their time average vanishes, $ \tau^{-1}\int_0^\tau e^{-\ii \omega t}\dd t \to 0 $ for $ \omega \neq 0 $; hence
\[
  \bar\rho := \lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^\tau\rho(t)\,\dd t
  = \sum_a\rho_{aa}\,|a\rangle\langle a| ,
\]
the \emph{diagonal ensemble}.
A measurement averaged over long times, or of a few-body observable at a typical late time in a macroscopic system (whose level spacings are so dense that the off-diagonal contributions add with effectively random phases), sees $ \bar\rho $ rather than $ \rho(t) $.
Because $ \ln\bar\rho $ is diagonal in the eigenbasis, $ \Tr[\rho(0)\ln\bar\rho] = \sum_a\rho_{aa}\ln\rho_{aa} = \Tr(\bar\rho\ln\bar\rho) $, and
\[
  S[\bar\rho] - S[\rho(0)] = D\big(\rho(0) \Vert \bar\rho\big) \;\geq\; 0 ,
\]
with equality only if $ \rho(0) $ was already diagonal.
This increase is only apparent.
The information is still there, in the phases of $ \rho_{ab} $, and an operation that reverses the sign of $ \hat H $ recovers it.
The nuclear spin echo (Hahn, 1950) does exactly this: nuclear spins precessing in an inhomogeneous field dephase and their net magnetization disappears; a $ \pi $ pulse reverses the precession, and the magnetization reappears, although the inhomogeneity was never known in detail --- only static.
What the echo cannot recover decays with the time $ T_2 $, set by the interactions among the spins and by fluctuating fields, which the pulse does not reverse.
The magic echo (Rhim, Pines and Waugh, 1971) reverses the dipolar interaction among the spins as well, and still recovers the signal only partially; the residual decay comes from the terms in the Hamiltonian that the reversal does not control.
Dephasing under the known Hamiltonian displaces information; the unknown part of the Hamiltonian removes it beyond recovery.

\paragraph{Correlations with the environment.}
The unknown $ \hat H' $ couples the system to degrees of freedom that nobody monitors.
Including those degrees of freedom makes the composite closed again, and then the information is not destroyed but transferred into correlations.
Let a system $ A $ interact with an environment $ B $, the composite evolving unitarily, and let $ \rho_A = \Tr_B\rho_{AB} $, $ \rho_B = \Tr_A\rho_{AB} $ be the reduced states, which are all that measurements on one part can access.
The mutual information
\[
  I(A{:}B) := S[\rho_A] + S[\rho_B] - S[\rho_{AB}] \;\geq\; 0
\]
is non-negative (subadditivity of the entropy, itself a case of Klein's inequality: $ I = D(\rho_{AB} \Vert \rho_A\otimes\rho_B) $).
Since $ S[\rho_{AB}] $ is conserved, $ S[\rho_A] + S[\rho_B] = S[\rho_{AB}] + I(A{:}B) $ grows by exactly the information stored in the correlations, including entanglement, that the interaction has built up.
Recovering it would require a joint measurement on $ A $ and on every degree of freedom of $ B $ that $ A $ has touched; once these include the walls and the radiation field, the information is lost as surely as if it had been destroyed.
For a small system and a large environment more is true (canonical typicality~\cite{Goldstein2006,Popescu2006}): for almost every \emph{pure} state of $ A + B $ in a narrow energy shell, $ \rho_A $ is close to $ e^{-\beta\hat H_A}/Z_A $, with $ \beta $ set by the shell.
The canonical state of a subsystem is what remains of its description once the environment is traced out.

\paragraph{Why the energy suffices: thermalization.}
The diagonal ensemble still remembers every $ \rho_{aa} $, i.e.\ exponentially many conserved quantities, and it is not the microcanonical state.
That few-body observables nevertheless take their microcanonical values, before $ \hat H' $ has had time to equalize the $ \rho_{aa} $, is the content of the \emph{eigenstate thermalization hypothesis}~\cite{Deutsch1991,Srednicki1994} (Appendix~\ref{app:eth}): the diagonal matrix elements $ \langle a|\hat O|a\rangle $ of a few-body observable are a smooth function $ O(E_a) $ of the energy, up to fluctuations exponentially small in the system size.
Then $ \Tr(\bar\rho\hat O) = \sum_a\rho_{aa}O(E_a) \simeq O(U) $ whenever $ \rho_{aa} $ is concentrated in an energy window narrow on the scale over which $ O $ varies, which is the case for any physically preparable state of a macroscopic system; and $ O(U) $ is also the microcanonical value.
The information carried by the $ \rho_{aa} $ beyond the energy is real, but invisible to few-body observables.
The hypothesis is a property of the Hamiltonian, well supported numerically for generic (chaotic) many-body systems.
It fails for integrable systems,\footnote{\label{fn:integrable}\textbf{Integrable systems.}
A classical system with $ f $ degrees of freedom is integrable (in the sense of Liouville) if it has $ f $ independent conserved quantities whose mutual Poisson brackets vanish; its motion is then confined to $ f $-dimensional invariant tori and is quasi-periodic, so it cannot explore the energy surface.
In quantum mechanics there is no single accepted definition.
In practice a many-body system is called integrable if it has an extensive set of local or quasi-local conserved operators that commute with $ \hat H $ and with each other: the occupations $ \hat n_\lambda $ of a quadratic Hamiltonian, or the hierarchies of interacting models solvable by the Bethe ansatz, such as the Heisenberg (XXZ) chain, the Lieb--Liniger Bose gas and the one-dimensional Hubbard model.
A practical signature is the level statistics: Poissonian for integrable systems, Wigner--Dyson for chaotic ones.} where more than the energy survives: they relax instead to a \emph{generalized} Gibbs ensemble with one multiplier for each conserved quantity.
It fails too for many-body localized systems, which do not thermalize at all.

\subsection{A test case: the two-level system}
\label{sec:found_tls}

The formalism of Sec.~\ref{sec:found_second} can be followed from beginning to end, with nothing left implicit, for a system of two levels: a spin $ 1/2 $ in a known field along $ z $, with level splitting $ \omega_0 $, and in an unknown additional field, produced for instance by the nuclear moments of the surrounding atoms.
The operator basis consists of the unit matrix and the three Pauli matrices $ \hat\sigma_x $, $ \hat\sigma_y $, $ \hat\sigma_z $.
The coefficient of the unit matrix shifts the zero of energy and drops out, so that
\[
  \hat H = \tfrac12\omega_0\,\hat\sigma_z , \qquad
  \hat H' = \tfrac12\,\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
  = \tfrac12\big(\eta_x\hat\sigma_x + \eta_y\hat\sigma_y + \eta_z\hat\sigma_z\big) :
\]
the unknown part of the Hamiltonian is three real numbers.
(The basis operators are here $ \hat\sigma_i/2 $, normalized to $ \Tr(\hat\sigma_i\hat\sigma_j)/4 = \delta_{ij}/2 $ rather than to unity, which only rescales the coefficients.)
Every density matrix is
\[
  \rho = \tfrac12\big(\mathbb{1} + \mathbf{s}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\big) , \qquad
  \mathbf{s} = \Tr(\rho\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}) , \qquad |\mathbf{s}| \leq 1 ,
\]
with the polarization $ \mathbf{s} $ (the Bloch vector) as its three parameters.
Its eigenvalues are $ (1 \pm s)/2 $ with $ s = |\mathbf{s}| $, so that the entropy depends on the length of $ \mathbf{s} $ alone,
\[
  S(s) = -\Big[\frac{1 + s}{2}\ln\frac{1 + s}{2} + \frac{1 - s}{2}\ln\frac{1 - s}{2}\Big] ,
\]
and decreases monotonically from $ \ln2 $ at $ s = 0 $ to zero for a pure state, $ s = 1 $.
The occupations of the upper and the lower level are $ (1 \pm s_z)/2 $, and the energy is $ U = \tfrac12\omega_0s_z $.

For given $ \boldsymbol{\eta} $ the von~Neumann equation with $ \hat H + \hat H'_\eta = \tfrac12\boldsymbol{\Omega}_\eta\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}} $ is
\[
  \frac{\dd\mathbf{s}_\eta}{\dd t} = \boldsymbol{\Omega}_\eta\times\mathbf{s}_\eta , \qquad
  \boldsymbol{\Omega}_\eta = \omega_0\,\mathbf{e}_z + \boldsymbol{\eta} ,
\]
a rigid rotation of the polarization about the total field.
It conserves $ |\mathbf{s}_\eta| $: this is the conservation of the entropy by the microscopic dynamics (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_unitary}).
Since $ \rho $ is linear in $ \mathbf{s} $, the description~\eqref{eq:prior_average} has the polarization $ \bar{\mathbf{s}}(t) = \int \dd^{3}\eta\;\pi(\boldsymbol{\eta})\,\mathbf{s}_\eta(t) $, and
\[
  |\bar{\mathbf{s}}(t)| \;\leq\; \int\!\dd^{3}\eta\;\pi(\boldsymbol{\eta})\,|\mathbf{s}_\eta(t)|
  = |\mathbf{s}(0)| .
\]
The inequality~\eqref{eq:prior_entropy} is here the triangle inequality: the average of vectors of equal length is shorter than each of them unless they all coincide.
The entropy of the description grows because the unknown field has turned the polarization by an unknown angle.

\paragraph{Static coefficients.}
Let $ \boldsymbol{\eta} $ be constant in time, with the flat prior~\eqref{eq:flat_prior} of width $ v $ in each component.
With $ \mathbf{n} = \boldsymbol{\Omega}_\eta/\Omega_\eta $ the axis of the rotation and $ \mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{s}(0) $,
\[
  \mathbf{s}_\eta(t) = (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{s}_0)\,\mathbf{n}
  + \big[\mathbf{s}_0 - (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{s}_0)\,\mathbf{n}\big]\cos\Omega_\eta t
  + \mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{s}_0\,\sin\Omega_\eta t .
\]
The first term is the part of the state that is diagonal in the eigenbasis of the full Hamiltonian.
The other two oscillate at a frequency that depends on $ \boldsymbol{\eta} $ and average to zero at long times, and the steady state~\eqref{eq:prior_steady} is $ \bar{\mathbf{s}}_\infty = \overline{(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{s}_0)\,\mathbf{n}} $.
Two limits can be worked out in closed form.

If the unknown field is weak, $ v \ll \omega_0 $, the axis is $ \mathbf{n} \simeq \mathbf{e}_z $ and $ \Omega_\eta \simeq \omega_0 + \eta_z $, up to corrections of relative order $ v^{2}/\omega_0^{2} $.
Then $ \bar s_z $ is constant, and the Gaussian average of $ e^{\ii \eta_zt} $ gives
\[
  \bar s_x(t) + \ii\bar s_y(t) = \big[s_x(0) + \ii s_y(0)\big]\,e^{\ii \omega_0t}\,e^{-v^{2}t^{2}/2} .
\]
The transverse polarization disappears in a time $ 1/v $ (the time $ T_2^{*} $ of magnetic resonance), with a Gaussian and not an exponential law, and the steady state is the diagonal ensemble of the known Hamiltonian, $ \bar{\mathbf{s}}_\infty = (0, 0, s_z(0)) $: the entropy has risen from $ S(|\mathbf{s}_0|) $ to $ S(|s_z(0)|) $.

If the known field is absent, $ \omega_0 = 0 $, the axis $ \mathbf{n} $ is distributed uniformly over the sphere, $ \overline{n_in_j} = \delta_{ij}/3 $, and the frequency $ \Omega_\eta = |\boldsymbol{\eta}| $ has a Maxwell distribution.
Since $ \overline{\sin(\Omega_\eta t)/\Omega_\eta t} = e^{-v^{2}t^{2}/2} $ for an isotropic Gaussian, a derivative of $ t $ times this expression with respect to $ t $ gives $ \overline{\cos\Omega_\eta t} $, and
\[
  \bar{\mathbf{s}}(t) = \mathbf{s}_0\Big[\frac13
  + \frac23\Big(1 - v^{2}t^{2}\Big)e^{-v^{2}t^{2}/2}\Big] ,
\]
the function of Kubo and Toyabe (1967), which is measured directly as the depolarization of muons implanted in a solid, where the unknown field is that of the randomly oriented nuclear moments.
After a minimum at $ t = \sqrt3/v $ the polarization recovers to one third of its initial value and stays there.
The steady state exists and its entropy $ S(s_0/3) $ is higher than the initial one, but it is lower than $ \ln2 $, and the state remembers the direction of $ \mathbf{s}_0 $: each realization conserves the component of the polarization along its own field, that is, its own energy, and one third is the prior average of $ n_z^{2} $.
A static unknown part does not erase everything that it could.
It is also reversible: a $ \pi $ pulse about a transverse axis at time $ t $ inverts the phase accumulated in the weak-field case and restores the transverse polarization at $ 2t $, for every $ \eta_z $ and without any knowledge of it (the spin echo of Sec.~\ref{sec:found_second}).

\paragraph{Fluctuating coefficients.}
Let the components of the unknown field fluctuate as white noise with rates $ \kappa_x $, $ \kappa_y $, $ \kappa_z $, $ \overline{\eta_i(t)\,\eta_j(t')} = \kappa_i\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t - t') $.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipator} with the operators $ \hat\sigma_i/2 $ is
\[
  \frac{\dd\varrho}{\dd t} = -\frac{\ii\omega_0}{2}[\hat\sigma_z, \varrho]
  - \frac18\sum_i\kappa_i\big[\hat\sigma_i, [\hat\sigma_i, \varrho]\big] ,
\]
and with $ [\hat\sigma_i, [\hat\sigma_i, \hat\sigma_j]] = 4(1 - \delta_{ij})\,\hat\sigma_j $ it becomes, for the polarization of the description (the bar is dropped),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bloch}
  \dot s_x = -\omega_0s_y - \frac{\kappa_y + \kappa_z}{2}\,s_x , \qquad
  \dot s_y = \omega_0s_x - \frac{\kappa_x + \kappa_z}{2}\,s_y , \qquad
  \dot s_z = -\frac{\kappa_x + \kappa_y}{2}\,s_z .
\end{equation}
Each component of the unknown field relaxes the two components of the polarization perpendicular to it.
For $ \kappa_x = \kappa_y = \kappa_\perp $ these are the equations that Bloch (1946) wrote down phenomenologically for nuclear magnetization, with the longitudinal and transverse relaxation times
\[
  \frac{1}{T_1} = \kappa_\perp , \qquad
  \frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{\kappa_\perp + \kappa_z}{2} = \frac{1}{2T_1} + \frac{1}{T_\varphi} ,
  \qquad \frac{1}{T_\varphi} = \frac{\kappa_z}{2} ,
\]
now expressed through the widths of the prior.
The decay is exponential and monotonic,
\[
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}|\mathbf{s}|^{2} = -(\kappa_y + \kappa_z)\,s_x^{2} - (\kappa_x + \kappa_z)\,s_y^{2}
  - (\kappa_x + \kappa_y)\,s_z^{2} \;\leq\; 0 ,
\]
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipator_entropy} for two levels, and no pulse brings the polarization back.
Compared with the static case the relaxation is slow: with $ \kappa \simeq 2v^{2}\tau_c $ the rate is $ v\times 2v\tau_c $, smaller than the static rate $ v $ by the small parameter of the white-noise limit (motional narrowing).
After the transients, $ t \gg T_1, T_2 $, the description is stationary, and which stationary state it reaches is decided by what the unknown part conserves.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{$ \hat H' $ conserves the energy}: only $ \eta_z $ is admitted, $ \kappa_\perp = 0 $. Then $ s_z $ is constant and $ s_x $, $ s_y $ decay in the time $ T_\varphi $. The steady state $ \varrho_\infty = \tfrac12(\mathbb{1} + s_z\hat\sigma_z) $ is the canonical state
  \[
    \varrho_\infty = \frac{e^{-\beta\hat H}}{Z} , \qquad
    \tanh\frac{\beta\omega_0}{2} = -s_z(0) = -\frac{2U}{\omega_0} ,
  \]
the state of largest entropy at the given energy (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_info}), with a temperature that is not a parameter of any reservoir but is fixed by the conserved energy $ U $ (and is negative if the initial state was inverted, $ s_z(0) > 0 $).
With two levels this costs nothing, since every state diagonal in the energy is a Gibbs state.
With more levels, dephasing leaves the diagonal ensemble, and transitions among the many states of an energy shell are needed to make it uniform there.
\item \emph{$ \hat H' $ conserves nothing}: $ \kappa_\perp > 0 $. The unknown transverse field does work on the spin, the energy is not conserved, and $ \mathbf{s} \to 0 $: the steady state is $ \varrho_\infty = \mathbb{1}/2 $, the uniform distribution over the $ W = 2 $ states, with $ S = \ln2 = \ln W $. For the flat prior, $ \kappa_x = \kappa_y = \kappa_z = \kappa $, Eq.~\eqref{eq:bloch} gives $ \mathbf{s}(t) = e^{-\kappa t}\,\mathbf{s}_{\rm rot}(t) $ with $ \mathbf{s}_{\rm rot} $ the polarization precessing under the known field alone, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:flat_relax} for $ W = 2 $ (in the normalized basis $ \hat\sigma_i/\sqrt2 $ the rate is $ \kappa/2 $, and $ \kappa W/2 = \kappa $).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Temperature from the conserved energy.}
Neither steady state has a temperature imposed on it, and none needs to be: a temperature appears, without any reservoir, as soon as there are many states at the same energy.
Take $ N $ identical two-level systems, isolated, $ \hat H = \tfrac12\omega_0\sum_k\hat\sigma_z^{(k)} $, with an unknown part that conserves the energy.
The admitted operators are those that commute with $ \hat H $, such as the Hermitian combinations of the flip-flop operators $ \hat\sigma_+^{(k)}\hat\sigma_-^{(l)} $, which pass an excitation from one system to another (for nuclear spins, the dipolar coupling between moments at unknown positions).
They act within the shell of the states with $ M $ systems in the upper level, of energy $ E = (M - N/2)\,\omega_0 $ and dimension $ W = \binom{N}{M} $.
With fluctuating coefficients and a flat prior on the shell, Eq.~\eqref{eq:flat_relax} gives the uniform distribution over the shell as the steady state, with $ S = \ln\binom{N}{M} $.
The state of any single system is then diagonal, and its upper level is occupied in the fraction $ \binom{N-1}{M-1}/\binom{N}{M} = M/N $ of the states of the shell, so that
\[
  \frac{p_\uparrow}{p_\downarrow} = \frac{M}{N - M} =: e^{-\beta\omega_0} :
\]
each two-level system is in a canonical state.
The $ \beta $ defined in this way is the thermodynamic one.
With $ p = M/N $ and Stirling's formula, $ S \simeq -N[p\ln p + (1 - p)\ln(1 - p)] $ and $ \dd E = N\omega_0\,\dd p $, hence
\[
  \frac{\partial S}{\partial E} = \frac{1}{\omega_0}\ln\frac{1 - p}{p} = \beta .
\]
The temperature is fixed by the conserved energy of the whole through $ 1/T = \partial S/\partial E $, and the prior contributes only the conservation law and the time that the relaxation takes.
This is the spin temperature of magnetic resonance.
If the unknown part does not conserve the energy, the steady state is uniform over all $ 2^{N} $ states, $ p = 1/2 $ and $ \beta = 0 $: a stochastic operator that does not commute with $ \hat H $ heats the body without limit.

\paragraph{What the test case shows.}
Under a Hamiltonian that is completely known the polarization rotates for ever and the entropy is constant.
With an unknown part, described by a prior over three coefficients, the description acquires a relaxation that no single realization has, the transients die out in the times $ 1/v $, $ T_2 $ and $ T_1 $, and the observables become stationary.
Their stationary values are those of an ensemble, and the ensemble is selected by the conservation laws of the unknown part: the canonical state at the conserved energy when $ \hat H' $ commutes with $ \hat H $, the uniform state when it does not, and for many two-level systems the canonical state of each at the temperature that the conserved energy of the whole defines.
In each case the entropy of the description has increased, by an amount that the prior fixes and that the known Hamiltonian could not have produced.

\subsection{A second test case: particles with an unknown one-body term}
\label{sec:found_anderson}

The two-level system has too few states to show what a static unknown part can fail to do.
The simplest many-particle case does: non-interacting particles hopping on a lattice of $ L $ sites, with an unknown part that is a generic one-body operator,
\[
  \hat H = -g\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\big(\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_j + \hat c_j^\dagger\hat c_i\big) ,
  \qquad
  \hat H' = \sum_{i,j}\eta_{ij}\,\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_j , \qquad \eta_{ji} = \eta_{ij}^{*} .
\]
The known part is the hopping with amplitude $ g $ between neighboring sites.
The unknown part is a stochastic hopping matrix: $ L $ real site energies $ \eta_{ii} $ and $ L(L-1)/2 $ complex amplitudes $ \eta_{ij} $, i.e.\ $ L^{2} $ real coefficients of the Hermitian basis operators $ \hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i $, $ \hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_j + \hat c_j^\dagger\hat c_i $ and $ \ii(\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_j - \hat c_j^\dagger\hat c_i) $.
All of them commute with the total number $ \hat N = \sum_i\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i $, which is therefore conserved; they do not commute with $ \hat H $.
What is known about $ \hat H' $ decides which of them are admitted, and the outcome depends on it.
Two cases are the extremes: an $ \hat H' $ that is \emph{local}, $ \eta_{ij} = \eta_i\delta_{ij} $, an unknown potential on the sites, and an $ \hat H' $ without any spatial structure, in which every pair of sites is admitted on the same footing.

Since both parts are quadratic, everything follows from one particle.
For given $ \eta $ the one-particle Hamiltonian is $ \hat h_\eta = -g\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}(|i\rangle\langle j| + |j\rangle\langle i|) + \sum_{i,j}\eta_{ij}|i\rangle\langle j| $, with eigenstates $ \phi_n^{\eta}(i) $, and a particle placed on site $ j $ is found on site $ i $ at time $ t $ with a probability that the description averages over the prior,
\[
  P_{ij}(t) = \overline{\big|\langle i|e^{-\ii \hat h_\eta t}|j\rangle\big|^{2}} ,
  \qquad
  P_{ij}(\infty) = \overline{\sum_n\big|\phi_n^{\eta}(i)\big|^{2}\,\big|\phi_n^{\eta}(j)\big|^{2}} ,
\]
the second expression being the steady state~\eqref{eq:prior_steady}.
The average is taken over probabilities and not over amplitudes: within each realization the amplitudes of all the paths from $ j $ to $ i $ interfere, with phases fixed by the definite, if unknown, values of the $ \eta_{ij} $.

\paragraph{Static coefficients, local $ \hat H' $: localization.}
Let only the site energies be admitted, $ \hat H' = \sum_i\eta_i\,\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i $, independent under the prior and of width $ v $.
This is the model of Anderson (1958), whose prior is flat in the literal sense, uniform on an interval $ [-v, v] $ for each site.
If the unknown potential were to act as the unknown part of Sec.~\ref{sec:found_second} is expected to, the particle would diffuse, $ P_{ij}(\infty) $ would be $ 1/L $ for every pair of sites, and the density would become uniform.
Anderson showed that it does not.
For sufficiently large $ v/g $ in three dimensions, and for any $ v > 0 $ in one and two (Mott and Twose, 1961; Abrahams, Anderson, Licciardello and Ramakrishnan, 1979), the eigenstates $ \phi_n^{\eta} $ are localized, each falling off exponentially from its own center over a localization length $ \ell_{\rm loc} $.
Then
\[
  P_{jj}(\infty) = \overline{\sum_n\big|\phi_n^{\eta}(j)\big|^{4}} \;>\; 0
  \qquad\text{as } L \to \infty ,
\]
and $ P_{ij}(\infty) $ decays exponentially with the distance between $ i $ and $ j $ on the scale $ \ell_{\rm loc} $: the particle stays, for ever, within a localization length of where it was put.
A steady state is reached and its entropy exceeds the initial one (Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior_entropy}), but it is not an equilibrium state.
An initial density profile never relaxes, the conductivity vanishes, and no ensemble with a few parameters describes the result.
This is the first reservation made in Sec.~\ref{sec:found_second}, in its extreme form.
Each realization has $ L $ conserved quantities, the occupations of its own eigenmodes $ \phi_n^{\eta} $, which are local and which differ from one realization to the next; the description remembers all of them.
Anderson's problem was in fact a failure of thermal relaxation, the absence of spin diffusion among donor spins in silicon, and the many-body localized systems mentioned in Sec.~\ref{sec:found_second} are its interacting descendants.

\paragraph{Static coefficients, structureless $ \hat H' $.}
Localization is a property of the local prior, not of static coefficients as such.
Let every pair of sites be admitted with the flat prior~\eqref{eq:flat_prior}, and let this part dominate the hopping, $ v\sqrt L \gg g $.
The matrix $ \eta_{ij} $ is then a member of Wigner's Gaussian ensemble, whose eigenvectors are distributed uniformly over all orthonormal bases, and the average over them is elementary,
\[
  P_{ij}(\infty) = \frac{1 + \delta_{ij}}{L + 1} .
\]
The particle is found on every other site with equal probability, and the density becomes uniform.
The return probability is twice that of any other site: a remnant of the interference, which the uniform distribution $ 1/L $ of a random walk lacks, and which vanishes relative to the total only as $ L \to \infty $.
Between the two extremes the range of the unknown amplitudes decides.
Random amplitudes between neighbors only still localize the particle in one dimension, and amplitudes that fall off as a power of the distance localize it if the power exceeds the dimension of the lattice and not otherwise.
Even where the density becomes uniform, the steady state of many particles is not thermal: each realization of a one-body $ \hat H' $ conserves the occupations of its $ L $ eigenmodes, and the description keeps their distribution.

Localization also marks the limit of the master equation of Sec.~\ref{sec:found_second}.
Applied to this model, the golden rule with the phases discarded at every interval gives transitions between plane waves at symmetric rates, hence diffusion and a uniform density, which is wrong.
For static coefficients the phases may not be discarded more than once: the amplitudes of a closed path and of the same path traversed in the opposite sense carry the same phase for every $ \eta $, they survive the average over the prior, and they enhance the return to the starting point.
The repeated random-phase assumption is legitimate only if the coefficients are renewed from one interval to the next.

\paragraph{Fluctuating coefficients: diffusion.}
Let the coefficients now fluctuate.
For the structureless $ \hat H' $, with the same rate $ \kappa $ for all the one-body operators, the one-particle density matrix obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:flat_relax} with $ W = L $: it relaxes to the uniform distribution over the sites at the rate $ \kappa L $, whatever the hopping.
The local case is the instructive one.
Let the site energies fluctuate as white noise, $ \overline{\eta_i(t)\,\eta_j(t')} = \kappa\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t - t') $, as they do when the potential is produced by a lattice that vibrates.
With $ \hat n_i = \hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i $, Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipator} has the dissipator $ -\tfrac12\kappa\sum_i[\hat n_i, [\hat n_i, \varrho]] $, and for the one-particle density matrix $ \varrho_{ij} $, with $ \hat n_i = |i\rangle\langle i| $ and $ \sum_i[\hat n_i, [\hat n_i, \varrho]] = 2\varrho - 2\sum_i\hat n_i\varrho\hat n_i $, it reads
\[
  \frac{\dd\varrho_{ij}}{\dd t} = -\ii[\hat h_0, \varrho]_{ij} - \kappa\,(1 - \delta_{ij})\,\varrho_{ij} ,
\]
with $ \hat h_0 $ the hopping alone: the coherences between different sites decay at the rate $ \kappa $, and the occupations $ n_i = \varrho_{ii} $ are not touched directly.
For $ \kappa \gg g $ the coherence between neighbors follows the occupations, $ \varrho_{i,i+1} \simeq (\ii g/\kappa)(n_{i+1} - n_i) $ on a chain, and inserted in $ \dot n_i = -\ii[\hat h_0, \varrho]_{ii} $ it gives
\[
  \frac{\dd n_i}{\dd t} = \frac{2g^{2}}{\kappa}\,\big(n_{i+1} + n_{i-1} - 2n_i\big) ,
\]
a random walk with the hopping rate $ 2g^{2}/\kappa $, i.e.\ diffusion.
The same equation holds for the density of many non-interacting particles.
It is a master equation with symmetric rates, its entropy grows monotonically, and its steady state is the uniform density $ n_i = N/L $.
For the full description the steady state must commute with every $ \hat n_i $ and with $ \hat H $, and is therefore uniform over all the configurations of $ N $ particles on $ L $ sites, with $ S = \ln\binom{L}{N} $ for fermions: the microcanonical state for the one quantity that the unknown part conserves.
The interference that localizes the particle has been removed, not by ignoring the phases, but because the fluctuating coefficients change them before a closed path is completed.
In a real solid the vibrations of the lattice do this, and a localized system conducts by hopping at any $ T > 0 $.

\paragraph{What the second test case shows.}
An unknown part of the Hamiltonian is necessary for the second law, but its being unknown is not sufficient, and what it does depends on which operators the prior admits.
With static coefficients the prior raises the entropy of the description and produces a steady state, and yet the body may fail to equilibrate, because every realization keeps its own extensive set of conserved quantities.
The thermodynamic steady state, a function of the few globally conserved quantities alone, is reached when the coefficients fluctuate in time, i.e.\ when the unknown part is a stochastic operator with a finite correlation time.
That requirement is the content of the protocol of Sec.~\ref{sec:found_protocol}.

\subsection{Entropy and chemical potential as devices}
\label{sec:found_info}

\paragraph{The device for energy exchange.}
The results of the three preceding subsections share one structure.
The unknown part of the Hamiltonian, whatever it is, drives the description to a stationary state that depends on it through the single parameter $ T $, and the approach is governed by a functional
\[
  F[\rho] = \Tr(\rho\hat H) - T\,S[\rho]
\]
that decreases monotonically: $ F[P] $ for the Brownian particle, $ F[p] $ for the master equation with a bath.
The first term is the known Hamiltonian.
The second, $ -T\,S[\rho] $, is all that survives of $ \hat H' $: the entropy, multiplied by $ T $, stands in for the unknown exchange of energy, and without it ($ T \to 0 $) the known dynamics would minimize $ \langle\hat H\rangle $ alone.
The details of $ \hat H' $ --- the friction $ \gamma $, the rates $ K_{ab} $ --- set how fast the minimum is approached, not where it lies; equilibrium thermodynamics keeps only $ T $.

\paragraph{The device for particle exchange.}
Particles enter and leave a body through processes at least as complex: tunneling through a contact, adsorption at a surface, a chemical reaction, the creation and annihilation of photons and phonons at the walls.
Let the transitions between the eigenstates $ |a\rangle $ (energies $ E_a $, particle numbers $ N_a $) now change $ N $ as well.
The reservoir with which particles are exchanged, being large, fixes the ratio of the forward and backward rates through two numbers: removing the energy $ E_a - E_b $ and $ N_a - N_b $ particles from it multiplies its number of states by $ e^{-\beta[(E_a - E_b) - \mu(N_a - N_b)]} $, with $ \mu $ the chemical potential of the reservoir.
Detailed balance becomes $ W_{ab}\,e^{-\beta\xi_b} = W_{ba}\,e^{-\beta\xi_a} $ with $ \xi_a = E_a - \mu N_a $, and the argument of the bath paragraph goes through with $ E_a $ replaced by $ \xi_a $: the description relaxes to $ \rho_{\rm G} = e^{-\beta(\hat H - \mu\hat N)}/\Xi $, and the functional
\[
  \Omega[\rho] = \Tr\big[\rho\,(\hat H - \mu\hat N)\big] - T\,S[\rho]
\]
decreases monotonically to $ \Omega = -T\ln\Xi $, with $ \Xi = \Tr e^{-\beta(\hat H - \mu\hat N)} $.
The particle-exchange Hamiltonian, whatever it is, has been replaced by a single term added to the known Hamiltonian, $ \hat{\mathcal{H}} = \hat H - \mu\hat N $: the simplest operator that can represent it, linear in the conserved quantity it exchanges, with $ \mu $ as its only parameter.
Three examples show how much is hidden in that term.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A quantum dot and a lead.} A level $ \varepsilon_d $ coupled by tunneling, $ \hat H_{\rm T} = \sum_k(t_k\hat c_k^\dagger\hat d + \text{h.c.}) $, to a metallic lead. The many electrons of the lead and the amplitudes $ t_k $ are replaced, for the equilibrium occupation of the dot in the weak-coupling limit, by the Fermi level $ \mu $ of the lead alone, $ \langle\hat n_d\rangle = 1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon_d - \mu)} + 1) $; the tunneling rate $ \Gamma = 2\pi\nu|t|^{2} $, with $ \nu $ the density of states of the lead at $ \mu $, plays the role of $ \gamma $ and sets only the time scale.
\item \emph{Photons in a cavity.} The walls absorb and emit photons, so $ \hat H' $ does not conserve their number, and no reservoir sets a price per photon. The photon number is not among the quantities that survive, there is no term to add, and $ \mu = 0 $: the Planck distribution. The same holds for phonons.
\item \emph{Chemical reactions.} A reaction that converts $ \nu_i $ particles of species $ i $ (with $ \nu_i < 0 $ for those consumed) leaves $ \sum_i\nu_i\hat N_i $ unchanged but no single $ \hat N_i $. The device assigns a $ \mu_i $ to each species, and stationarity of the reaction requires $ \sum_i\nu_i\mu_i = 0 $, the law of mass action.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The general rule.}
Each channel through which a conserved quantity $ \hat X $ is exchanged with uncontrolled surroundings is represented by a term $ -\lambda\hat X $ added to the Hamiltonian, with $ \lambda $ the conjugate intensive variable that the surroundings fix ($ \lambda = \mu $ for $ \hat X = \hat N $).
Each such channel moves the description from one statistical ensemble to the next (microcanonical, canonical, grand canonical); on the thermodynamic side it is one Legendre transform.
The energy itself is the exception: adding a term $ -\lambda\hat H $ would only rescale the Hamiltonian, so the unknown exchange of energy cannot be represented by an operator.
It is represented instead by the noise, and its imprint is the term $ -T\,S[\rho] $, which depends on the state and not on any observable.
The two devices are different in kind, and together they give the grand canonical state.

\paragraph{Shannon's measure, and Jaynes' reading.}
The form of the device is Shannon's.
$ S[\rho] = -\Tr(\rho\ln\rho) $ has an operational meaning: $ S/\ln2 $ is the number of yes/no questions needed, on average and per copy in the limit of many copies, to identify the microstate of a system known only to be in the state $ \rho $.
In the device it measures how widely the unknown part of the Hamiltonian has spread the state over the microstates of the known part.
The same state $ \rho_{\rm G} $ is also the state of largest $ S $ among all states with the same $ \langle\hat H\rangle = U $ and $ \langle\hat N\rangle = \bar N $~\cite{Jaynes1957}, and the proof needs no calculus of variations.
Since $ \ln\rho_{\rm G} = -\beta(\hat H - \mu\hat N) - \ln\Xi $ is \emph{linear} in $ \hat H $ and $ \hat N $, every state $ \rho $ with the same averages has
\[
  \Tr(\rho\ln\rho_{\rm G}) = -\beta(U - \mu\bar N) - \ln\Xi = \Tr(\rho_{\rm G}\ln\rho_{\rm G}) ,
\]
and therefore
\[
  S[\rho_{\rm G}] - S[\rho] = \big[\Tr(\rho\ln\rho) - \Tr(\rho\ln\rho_{\rm G})\big]
  = D(\rho \Vert \rho_{\rm G}) \;\geq\; 0 ,
\]
with equality only for $ \rho = \rho_{\rm G} $, by Klein's inequality.
Read as inference, this says that $ \rho_{\rm G} $ is the least committal assignment given the conserved averages.
Read as physics, it says that the unknown part of the Hamiltonian has erased everything except the conserved quantities.
Only the second reading explains why the assignment predicts experiments: the inference alone would apply equally to a closed system with a completely known Hamiltonian, where the ignored details persist and could be recovered by reversing the dynamics (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_unitary}).

\paragraph{The meaning of $ T $ and $ \mu $.}
Neither parameter is a property of the known Hamiltonian; both are properties of the surroundings to which the unknown part couples, and they are all that equilibrium thermodynamics retains of that part.
$ T $ is the strength of the unknown kicks, in the precise sense of the fluctuation--dissipation relation, which fixes the strength of the noise at $ 2\gamma T $ for a given friction $ \gamma $; equivalently, it fixes the ratio $ e^{-\beta(E_a - E_b)} $ of the rates up and down in energy.
$ \mu $ is the price per particle in the ratio of the rates of uptake and release.
For a macroscopic system the distinction between a closed body and one that exchanges energy or particles hardly matters for the potentials, because the distributions of $ E $ and $ N $ in the canonical and grand canonical states have relative widths $ O(N^{-1/2}) $ (equivalence of the ensembles).

\subsection{Equilibrium as a protocol}
\label{sec:found_protocol}

\paragraph{Separation of time scales.}
The devices describe a stationary state, and a stationary state is reached only after the relaxation processes driven by $ \hat H' $ have run their course.
``Equilibrium'' is therefore less a property of a state found in nature than a protocol: prepare the body, wait until the complex relaxation processes have happened, and then measure on a longer time scale still.
Three time scales enter.
The correlation time $ \tau_c $ of the stochastic force (for the Brownian particle, the duration of a molecular collision, about $ 10^{-13} $ s) must be short enough for the force to be represented as white noise; otherwise the friction acquires a memory kernel, which changes the kinetics but not the stationary state.
The relaxation time $ \tau_{\rm rel} $ of the retained variables ($ m/\gamma $ and the diffusion time across the well for the Brownian particle, the inverse of the rates $ K_{ab} $ in the master equation) sets how long one must wait.
And the observation time $ \tau_{\rm obs} $ over which a measurement averages must exceed $ \tau_{\rm rel} $, so that the measurement records stationary averages rather than individual kicks:
\[
  \tau_c \;\ll\; \tau_{\rm rel} \;\ll\; t_{\rm wait},\ \tau_{\rm obs} .
\]
Processes slower than the protocol are not part of the device at all.
They are frozen, and the variables they would change enter as internal constraints: the ortho and para forms of molecular hydrogen, which without a catalyst interconvert over days or longer; nuclear spins in an insulator at low temperature, whose spin--lattice relaxation can take hours; the configurational degrees of freedom of a glass below $ T_g $.
The same body can be in equilibrium for one protocol and not for another, and the division of the Hamiltonian into $ \hat H $ and $ \hat H' $ is made relative to the time scales of the protocol.
In Feynman's phrase, equilibrium is reached when all the fast things have happened and all the slow things have not.

\paragraph{Entropy as a clock.}
The known Hamiltonian cannot order events: every function of $ \rho $ is conserved under $ \hat H $ alone (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_unitary}).
The unknown part can.
Under the master equation $ S[p] $ grows monotonically and stops at the stationary state, so $ S $ itself, or $ -F $ with a bath, is an internal clock that runs only while $ \hat H' $ acts and reads ``equilibrium'' when it stops; the waiting time $ t_{\rm wait} $ of the protocol is the statement that this clock has run out.
Barontini \cite{Barontini} realized the construction with an isolated condensate split by a barrier into an observed and an unobserved sector: the accumulated change of the coarse-grained entropy of the observed sector, $ \tau \propto \int|\dd S| $, orders its expansions and recollapses while the entropy of the whole stays constant, and $ \tau $ freezes when the exchange across the barrier stops.
Two caveats.
The clock uses $ |\dd S| $, so it runs in both directions of entropy flow and is an ordering parameter rather than an arrow; and on the time scale of the experiment the exchange is reversible, so it measures the amount of exchange through the coupling, not information lost.

\paragraph{No assumption that the coupling is weak.}
The derivation of the ensembles from a reservoir treats system and reservoir as weakly coupled: the interaction energy at their interface, which grows as its area, is neglected against the bulk energies, which grow as the volumes, so that the energies add and the states of the reservoir can be counted separately.
The definition of heat in Sec.~\ref{sec:found_heat} and the bath paragraph of Sec.~\ref{sec:found_second} rest on the same assumption.
The device does not need it.
The stochastic force represents the unknown part through its effect on the retained variables, whatever the strength of the coupling: in the Langevin equation $ \gamma $ may be as large as one likes, and the stationary distribution remains $ P \propto e^{-\mathcal{V}/T} $; the strength of the coupling sets the kinetics, not the end point.
When a strong coupling also modifies the retained degrees of freedom statically --- a polaronic shift of a level, the screening of an interaction, the energy of an interface --- the modification is absorbed into the known part, which becomes an effective, in general temperature-dependent, Hamiltonian $ \hat H_{\rm eff} $ (the Hamiltonian of mean force), and the stationary state is $ e^{-\beta\hat H_{\rm eff}}/Z_{\rm eff} $.
Surface and interface interactions therefore need not be negligible compared with the bulk.
What their size decides is not whether the ensemble applies but whether the resulting potentials are extensive: in a nanoscale body, or across an interface, they survive as surface free energies without invalidating the description.

\subsection{Clausius and Kelvin from reversible dynamics}
\label{sec:found_statements}

The classical statements of the second law can be derived from the microscopic dynamics with no assumption about irreversibility at all.
Two ingredients suffice: the evolution of the whole is unitary, for any Hamiltonian whatever and however violently it is varied in time; and the bodies start in \emph{uncorrelated Gibbs states}.
The arrow of time is entirely in the second ingredient.

\paragraph{One identity.}
Let a body $ B $ with Hamiltonian $ \hat H_B $ start in the Gibbs state $ \gamma_B = e^{-\beta\hat H_B}/Z_B $ and end, after any process, in a state $ \rho_B' $, which need not be a Gibbs state.
With $ Q_B \equiv \Tr(\rho_B'\hat H_B) - \Tr(\gamma_B\hat H_B) $ the energy it has received and $ \Delta S_B = S[\rho_B'] - S[\gamma_B] $,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gibbs_identity}
  \beta\,Q_B = \Delta S_B + D\big(\rho_B'\,\big\|\,\gamma_B\big)
\end{equation}
The proof is two lines.
Since $ \ln\gamma_B = -\beta\hat H_B - \ln Z_B $,
\[
  D(\rho_B' \Vert \gamma_B) = \Tr(\rho_B'\ln\rho_B') - \Tr(\rho_B'\ln\gamma_B)
  = -S[\rho_B'] + \beta\Tr(\rho_B'\hat H_B) + \ln Z_B ,
\]
and the same expression with $ \rho_B' = \gamma_B $ vanishes, $ 0 = -S[\gamma_B] + \beta\Tr(\gamma_B\hat H_B) + \ln Z_B $; subtracting gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:gibbs_identity}.
By Klein's inequality $ D \geq 0 $, so a body that starts in equilibrium obeys $ \Delta S_B \leq Q_B/T $ for \emph{every} process: its entropy cannot grow by more than the heat it receives divided by its initial temperature.
The excess $ D(\rho_B' \Vert \gamma_B) $ is the entropy that the body itself creates by being driven away from its Gibbs state.
For a large body that remains close to a Gibbs state at a slightly shifted temperature it is of second order, $ D \simeq Q_B^{2}/2T^{2}C_B $, and it vanishes for an ideal reservoir, $ C_B \to \infty $: this is the defining property of an ideal reservoir, that it exchanges heat without producing entropy, now derived.

\paragraph{The Clausius inequality and the entropy produced.}
Let a system $ A $, in an arbitrary state $ \rho_A $, be brought into contact with a bath $ B $ in the Gibbs state $ \gamma_B $ at temperature $ T $, the two uncorrelated, $ \rho_{AB} = \rho_A\otimes\gamma_B $.
The composite evolves unitarily under $ \hat H_A(\lambda(t)) + \hat H_B + \hat H_{\rm int}(t) $, the coupling being switched off at the end, and $ Q \equiv -Q_B $ is the heat received by $ A $ (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_heat}).
Unitary evolution conserves $ S[\rho_{AB}] $, and the composite starts uncorrelated, so the sum of the entropies of the parts grows by exactly the mutual information that the process builds up, $ \Delta S_A + \Delta S_B = I'(A{:}B) $ (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_second}).
Combined with Eq.~\eqref{eq:gibbs_identity} for the bath,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_info}
  \sigma\;\equiv\;\Delta S_A - \frac{Q}{T}
  \;=\; I'(A{:}B) + D\big(\rho_B'\,\big\|\,\gamma_B\big)\;\geq\;0\
\end{equation}
This is the Clausius inequality $ \Delta S_A \geq Q/T $, with the entropy produced $ \sigma $ given by an exact formula (Esposito, Lindenberg and Van den Broeck, 2010).
It is the sum of two terms, each a quantity of information, and neither is accessible to the macroscopic controls: the correlations that the process has built between the system and the bath, and the displacement of the bath from its Gibbs state, which is spread over its unmonitored degrees of freedom.
A process is reversible when both vanish, and that is the precise sense in which the entropy produced is the information moved out of reach of the macroscopic controls.
If the final state of $ A $ is not itself a maximum-entropy state, its thermodynamic entropy exceeds $ S[\rho_A'] $ (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_law}), and the inequality holds a fortiori for the thermodynamic $ \Delta S $.

\paragraph{Kelvin's statement: Gibbs states are passive.}
Let a system in contact with a single bath be driven through a cycle: the control parameters $ \lambda(t) $ return to their initial values at the end, so that the Hamiltonian $ \hat H_{AB}(\lambda) $ of system and bath together is the same at the beginning and at the end.
Let system and bath start in joint equilibrium at temperature $ T $, $ \gamma_{AB} = e^{-\beta\hat H_{AB}}/Z_{AB} $.
The composite is closed except for the agent, so the work done on it is $ W = \Tr(\rho_{AB}'\hat H_{AB}) - \Tr(\gamma_{AB}\hat H_{AB}) $, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:gibbs_identity} applied to the composite as a single body, with $ \Delta S_{AB} = 0 $ by unitarity, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:passivity}
  \beta\,W = D\big(\rho_{AB}'\,\big\|\,\gamma_{AB}\big) \;\geq\; 0 .
\end{equation}
No cycle, however it is designed, extracts work from a system in equilibrium with a single bath: Gibbs states are \emph{passive}~\cite{PuszWoronowicz1978,Lenard1978}.
Equality requires $ \rho_{AB}' = \gamma_{AB} $, i.e.\ a cycle that leaves everything as it was.
The only assumption is the initial Gibbs state, and it is indispensable.
For a Gibbs state with $ \beta < 0 $, a population-inverted state, the same identity gives $ W \leq 0 $: work can be extracted from it by a cycle, and such a state is not an equilibrium state.

\paragraph{Clausius' statement.}
Let two bodies $ A $ and $ B $ start in Gibbs states at temperatures $ T_A $ and $ T_B $, uncorrelated, and let them interact in a way that exchanges energy between them without supplying any, i.e.\ by a unitary that commutes with $ \hat H_A + \hat H_B $.
Then the heat received by $ A $ is $ Q = Q_A = -Q_B $.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:gibbs_identity} for each body, $ \beta_AQ = \Delta S_A + D_A $ and $ -\beta_BQ = \Delta S_B + D_B $, added and combined with $ \Delta S_A + \Delta S_B = I'(A{:}B) $, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clausius_micro}
  \big(\beta_A - \beta_B\big)\,Q \;=\; I'(A{:}B) + D_A + D_B \;\geq\; 0 .
\end{equation}
If $ T_A < T_B $, so that $ \beta_A > \beta_B $, then $ Q \geq 0 $: the heat flows into the colder body (Partovi, 2008; Jennings and Rudolph, 2010).
This is Clausius' statement, obtained from the reversible dynamics of the two bodies and nothing else.

\paragraph{Where the arrow is.}
The three results use unitary, hence reversible, dynamics, and they are not time-symmetric; the asymmetry lies entirely in the assumption that the bodies start uncorrelated and in Gibbs states.
Run the microscopic dynamics backwards from the final state, and the reversed process starts with correlations $ I'(A{:}B) > 0 $ and with a bath out of its Gibbs state; neither assumption holds, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:clausius_micro} no longer forbids heat to flow from the colder body to the hotter.
With initial correlations prepared deliberately this has been observed: two nuclear spins in a molecule, initially correlated, exchange heat from cold to hot while their correlations are consumed (Micadei et al., 2019).
The second law is therefore not a property of the dynamics but of the states in which bodies are found before they interact, and those are supplied by the unknown part of the Hamiltonian (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_second}): it erases the correlations left by earlier interactions and returns each body to a Gibbs state, so that every new process starts under the assumption that the derivations require.

\subsection{Maxwell's demon and Landauer's principle}
\label{sec:found_demon}

\paragraph{The demon.}
Maxwell (1867) imagined a being that operates a frictionless trap door in a wall dividing a gas at uniform temperature, opening it for fast molecules moving one way and for slow ones moving the other.
It does no work, yet it produces a temperature difference from which work can be extracted, and the entropy of the gas decreases.
Szilard (1929) reduced the paradox to its essentials, a single molecule in a box of volume $ V $ in contact with a bath at temperature $ T $.
Insert a partition in the middle, which costs no work; find out which half contains the molecule, one bit of information; and let the molecule push the partition, now used as a piston, isothermally back to the empty end.
The work delivered is
\[
  W_{\rm out} = \int_{V/2}^{V}\!P\,\dd V' = T\int_{V/2}^{V}\frac{\dd V'}{V'} = T\ln2 ,
\]
supplied as heat by the bath.
The gas is back in its initial state, the bath has lost entropy $ \ln2 $, and if nothing else had changed, Kelvin's statement, which Sec.~\ref{sec:found_statements} derived from the microscopic dynamics, would be violated.

\paragraph{The memory.}
Something else has changed: the outcome of the measurement is stored in a physical memory $ M $, a system with two macroscopically distinct sets of microstates, the logical states $ 0 $ and $ 1 $, taken of equal internal entropy.
Measurement is a copy.
Starting from $ M $ in the state $ 0 $, it maps (molecule left, $ 0 $) to (left, $ 0 $) and (right, $ 0 $) to (right, $ 1 $), a one-to-one map of the joint states that can be realized unitarily and, as Bennett (1982) showed, in principle without any expenditure of work.
After the measurement the molecule and the memory share the mutual information $ I = \ln2 $; the work extraction consumes it, leaving the molecule spread over the whole box again and uncorrelated with the record, while the memory is left in $ 0 $ or $ 1 $ with equal probabilities and entropy $ \ln2 $.
The entropy lost by the bath has gone into the memory, and the total has not decreased.
The engine has not completed a cycle; the memory has not been returned to its initial state.

\paragraph{Landauer's principle.}
To work cyclically the demon must reset its memory to $ 0 $ whatever it holds.
This \emph{erasure} is a many-to-one map of the logical states, and since unitary evolution is one-to-one, it cannot be carried out on the memory alone: it must be carried out by a unitary acting on the memory together with an environment, which is left holding the information.
Let the environment be a bath at temperature $ T $, initially in its Gibbs state and uncorrelated with the memory.
Equation~\eqref{eq:sigma_info} with $ A = M $ gives $ Q \leq T\,\Delta S_M $ for the heat received by the memory, i.e.\ for the heat delivered to the bath,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:landauer}
  Q_{\rm bath}\;\geq\;-T\,\Delta S_M\;=\;T\,H(p) ,\qquad
  H(p) = -p\ln p - (1-p)\ln(1-p) ,\
\end{equation}
where $ p $ is the probability that the memory held $ 1 $ and $ \Delta S_M = -H(p) $ is the entropy removed from it, the internal entropies of the two logical states being equal.
For an unknown bit, $ p = 1/2 $, the bound is $ T\ln2 $ (Landauer, 1961).
Equality is approached by erasing quasi-statically, for instance by lowering the barrier of a double well slowly, tilting it towards $ 0 $ and raising it again, and the bound has been measured in that form with a colloidal particle in a double-well optical trap, the mean dissipated heat approaching $ T\ln2 $ for slow erasure (B\'erut et al., 2012).
Landauer's principle is therefore not an additional postulate: it is the Clausius inequality applied to a memory, once the uncertainty about its logical state is counted, as the Gibbs entropy counts it, as part of its entropy.

\paragraph{The balance.}
Over a full cycle of Szilard's engine the bath supplies $ T\ln2 $ as work and receives at least $ T\ln2 $ as heat when the memory is erased; the net work delivered is $ W_{\rm out} \leq 0 $, and Kelvin's statement holds for engine, memory and bath together.
The cost is in the erasure, not, as Szilard and Brillouin (1951) supposed, in the measurement.
A demon that never erased would instead fill its memory with entropy $ \ln2 $ per cycle; a tape of blank bits is a fuel, which the demon converts into work at the rate of at most $ T\ln2 $ per bit.
The general form of the balance, for any measurement that leaves a mutual information $ I $ between system and memory followed by feedback, bounds the work delivered by a system in contact with a bath at $ T $ as
\[
  W_{\rm out} \;\leq\; -\Delta F + T\,I
\]
(Sagawa and Ueda, 2008), with $ \Delta F = 0 $, $ I = \ln2 $ for Szilard's engine; the conversion of information into work at this rate has been realized with a Brownian particle driven by feedback (Toyabe et al., 2010).

\paragraph{What the demon shows.}
The demon is an agent for which part of the unknown Hamiltonian is known: it follows individual molecules.
This is precisely what the macroscopic constraints of the second law exclude, and the paradox disappears as soon as the agent is included in the system, because its knowledge is stored in a physical memory whose entropy enters the balance.
Information about the microstate is a thermodynamic resource, worth at most $ T\ln2 $ of work per bit, and acquiring it, using it and discarding it is subject to the same inequality as any other exchange of energy.
This is the sense in which the entropy, a measure of what is not known about the microstate, is a physical quantity: what is known can be turned into work, and forgetting it costs heat.

\subsection{The second law as a statement about information}
\label{sec:found_law}

Assembled, the picture is this.
Microscopic dynamics under any completely known Hamiltonian destroys no information: the Gibbs entropy of the full state and its entire energy distribution are conserved.
The second law arises because the description of a macroscopic body never contains its whole Hamiltonian.
The unknown part $ \hat H' $, whether external couplings or the body's own unresolved degrees of freedom, acts on the microstate far below the resolution of any measurement, conserves the energy and the particle number except through the channels represented by $ T $ and $ \mu $, and scrambles everything else.
Its coefficients in the operator basis are known only through a prior, and the description is the average, over that prior, of the unitarily evolved states (Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior_average}).
The average obeys the von~Neumann equation of the known part with a dissipator added (Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipator}), which can only increase $ S[\rho] $; in the steady state only the conserved quantities remain, and the state is the one that maximizes $ S $ given them.
The thermodynamic entropy is the entropy of that state,
\[
  S_{\rm th}(\mathcal{A}) := \max\big\{\,S[\rho] \;:\; \rho \text{ reproduces the macroscopic data } \mathcal{A}\,\big\} ,
\]
which for $ \mathcal{A} = (U, V, N) $ is the equilibrium entropy of statistical mechanics.
In this form the second law has a two-line proof.
Prepare the system in equilibrium under some internal constraint, so that its state $ \rho(0) $ is the maximiser for the initial data $ \mathcal{A}_0 $ and $ S[\rho(0)] = S_{\rm th}(\mathcal{A}_0) $.
Release the constraint and wait, as the protocol of Sec.~\ref{sec:found_protocol} prescribes, until the system has evolved to new macroscopic data $ \mathcal{A}_1 $.
The evolved state $ \rho(t) $ reproduces $ \mathcal{A}_1 $ (these are its own expectation values), so it is one of the candidates in the maximum defining $ S_{\rm th}(\mathcal{A}_1) $; hence
\[
  S_{\rm th}(\mathcal{A}_1) \;\geq\; S[\rho(t)] \;\geq\; S[\rho(0)] = S_{\rm th}(\mathcal{A}_0) .
\]
The middle inequality is an equality for exactly unitary evolution (Sec.~\ref{sec:found_unitary}), and holds in general once the prior over the coefficients of the unknown part of the Hamiltonian is included (Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior_entropy}).
The inequality is the entropy form of the minimum principles of thermodynamics, and the same argument with a bath gives the free energy.

The proof shows only that $ S_{\rm th} $ cannot decrease.
That it actually increases, and that the increase is physical rather than a matter of bookkeeping, is the content of Sec.~\ref{sec:found_second}.
Under exactly unitary evolution $ \rho(t) $ differs from the maximiser for $ \mathcal{A}_1 $, and the difference --- the information in phases and correlations --- could in principle be recovered by reversing the dynamics.
The unknown part of the Hamiltonian removes that possibility: it erases the difference faster than any experiment could exploit it, so that $ \rho(t) $ becomes, for every feasible measurement and every feasible reversal, the maximum-entropy state itself.
Equality holds only if no information has been moved out of reach of the macroscopic observables --- which is the microscopic meaning of a reversible process.

The entropy is therefore neither a property of the microstate nor a product of the microscopic equations of the known Hamiltonian.
It is the device by which a description that cannot contain the whole Hamiltonian accounts for the part it leaves out, as the term $ -\mu\hat N $ accounts for the unknown exchange of particles and as friction and noise account for the unknown kicks of the fluid on a Brownian particle.
Shannon's formula gives the device its form; the unknown part of the Hamiltonian gives it its content; and the second law states that this part, which conserves the energy and scrambles the rest, can only increase it.
Neither the energy variance nor any other property of the energy distribution can play the role of the entropy: the variance is conserved in an isolated system, and in a body cooled by a bath it falls, from $ T_1^{2}C(T_1) $ to $ T_2^{2}C(T_2) $ when $ C $ does not decrease with $ T $, while the total entropy grows.

\section{Thermalization of a Whole and of a Part}
\label{sec:defs}

\subsection{Invariants of Hamiltonian dynamics}
\label{sec:invariants}

A classical system with phase-space point $ \Gamma $ and Hamiltonian $ H $ is described by a probability density $ \rho(\Gamma,t) $ obeying the Liouville equation $ \partial_t \rho = \{ H , \rho \} $, where $ \{\cdot,\cdot\} $ is the Poisson bracket.
Its solution is $ \rho(\Gamma,t) = \rho(\Phi_{-t}\Gamma, 0) $, with $ \Phi_t $ the Hamiltonian flow, and $ \Phi_t $ preserves phase-space volume (Liouville's theorem).
Let $ \rho_{\mathrm{eq}} = f(H) $ be any stationary density, for instance the Gibbs density $ e^{-\beta H}/Z $ with $ \beta = 1/T $.
Since $ H(\Phi_{-t}\Gamma) = H(\Gamma) $, also $ \rho_{\mathrm{eq}}(\Gamma) = \rho_{\mathrm{eq}}(\Phi_{-t}\Gamma) $, and the change of variables $ \Gamma \to \Phi_{-t}\Gamma $, whose Jacobian is unity, shows that the following three quantities are independent of time:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  S_{\mathrm{G}}[\rho_t] = -\int \dd\Gamma \, \rho_t \ln \rho_t ,
  \qquad
  D(\rho_t \Vert \rho_{\mathrm{eq}}) = \int \dd\Gamma \, \rho_t \ln \frac{\rho_t}{\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}} ,
  \\
  \Vert \rho_t - \rho_{\mathrm{eq}} \Vert_1 = \int \dd\Gamma \, \big| \rho_t - \rho_{\mathrm{eq}} \big| .
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:invariants}
\end{equation}
The first is the Gibbs entropy.
The second, the relative entropy\footnote{The relative entropy (Kullback--Leibler divergence) $ D(\rho\Vert\sigma) $ is non-negative and vanishes only for $ \rho = \sigma $. It measures how well $ \rho $ can be distinguished from $ \sigma $ by sampling.}, and the third, the $ L^1 $ (total-variation) distance, measure how far the state is from equilibrium.
In quantum mechanics $ \hat\rho_t = \hat U_t \hat\rho_0 \hat U_t^\dagger $ with $ \hat U_t = e^{-\ii \hat H t} $, and a stationary state commutes with $ \hat H $.
The von Neumann entropy $ -\Tr \hat\rho \ln \hat\rho $, the relative entropy $ \Tr \hat\rho (\ln\hat\rho - \ln\hat\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}) $ and the trace distance $ \Tr | \hat\rho_t - \hat\rho_{\mathrm{eq}} | $ are invariant for the same reason: they are unchanged by a common unitary transformation of both arguments.

It follows that a closed system never approaches a Gibbs state as a whole.
If the initial state differs from every Gibbs state, it keeps its distance for all times, whether the dynamics is integrable or chaotic.
This is what is meant below by the statement that a closed system does not thermalize as a whole.

\subsection{Reduced descriptions}

Thermodynamics refers to a reduced description, of one of two kinds.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item The state of a subsystem $ S $ of a composite system $ S + B $: the marginal density $ \rho_S = \int \dd\Gamma_B \, \rho $, or the reduced density matrix $ \hat\rho_S = \Tr_B \hat\rho $.
\item The expectation values of a few observables $ A_1, \dots, A_n $, such as the energy of a region or the occupation of a level.
\end{itemize}
Neither is subject to Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariants}.
The entropies are related by
\begin{equation}
  S[\rho] = S[\rho_S] + S[\rho_B] - I(S\!:\!B) ,
  \qquad I(S\!:\!B) \ge 0 ,
  \label{eq:mutual}
\end{equation}
where $ I $ is the mutual information, which quantifies the correlations between $ S $ and $ B $.
At constant $ S[\rho] $, the sum of the subsystem entropies can grow only as fast as the correlations grow.
The entropy produced in a reduced description is therefore information transferred into correlations with the discarded degrees of freedom, not information destroyed.

A reduced description thermalizes if, for a class of initial states, it approaches the corresponding reduction of a Gibbs state of the whole and stays close to it for most later times.
Two weaker properties must be distinguished from this.
Equilibration means that the reduced description approaches some stationary value.
If the dynamics conserves quantities $ I_k $ in addition to the energy, their initial values are retained, and the stationary state reached is the generalized Gibbs ensemble (GGE)~\cite{Rigol2007}
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mathrm{GGE}} = Z^{-1} \exp\Big( - \sum_k \lambda_k I_k \Big) ,
  \label{eq:gge}
\end{equation}
with the multipliers $ \lambda_k $ fixed by the initial values of the $ I_k $.
It coincides with a Gibbs state only if $ \lambda_k I_k $ sums to $ \beta H $.

Systems with as many independent conserved quantities as degrees of freedom are called integrable, and systems with only a few, such as the energy, the particle number and the momentum, non-integrable.
Appendix~\ref{app:integrability} gives the precise definitions for classical and quantum systems, and the level-statistics test used in Sec.~\ref{sec:mz}.

\subsection{What it means to be thermal}
\label{sec:thermal-criteria}

Fitting a Gibbs distribution to one measured statistic is a weak test of thermalization.
Any two populations define a temperature through their ratio, and a distribution over a few levels can usually be fitted once a temperature and a chemical potential are free.
Different observables may then return different temperatures.
The harmonic chain of Sec.~\ref{sec:chain} is an example: by Eq.~\eqref{eq:chain-local} each site has its own kinetic temperature, and in a GGE each conserved quantity carries its own multiplier $ \lambda_k $.
Glasses and driven systems show effective temperatures that depend on the time scale of the measurement~\cite{Cugliandolo2011}.
Several temperatures are therefore not a paradox but a diagnosis: the state is not a Gibbs state.
A definition of thermalization must exclude them by construction.
Three criteria of increasing strength do so.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(T\arabic*), leftmargin=3.0em]
\item \label{t:fixed} The temperature is fixed, not fitted. The reference is the Gibbs state of the whole with the same mean values of the conserved quantities, e.g.\ $ \langle H \rangle_\beta = E $, and, if particles are exchanged, the chemical potential fixed by $ \langle N \rangle = N $. The comparison then has no free parameter.
\item \label{t:state} The state, not a statistic, is compared. For a subsystem $ S $ the criterion is the trace distance
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal D_S(t) = \tfrac12 \Tr \big| \hat\rho_S(t) - \Tr_B \hat\rho_\beta \big| ,
  \qquad
  \big| \langle \hat O \rangle_t - \langle \hat O \rangle_\beta \big| \le 2 \Vert \hat O \Vert \, \mathcal D_S(t) ,
  \label{eq:trace-criterion}
\end{equation}
which bounds the deviation of every observable $ \hat O $ of $ S $ at once ($ \Vert\hat O\Vert $ is its largest eigenvalue in magnitude). If $ \mathcal D_S $ is small, all observables of $ S $, including all correlations between its parts, show the same temperature. Observable-dependent temperatures are then excluded.
\item \label{t:kms} The dynamics obeys detailed balance. Static criteria can be met by a non-thermal state whose one-point functions happen to agree with the Gibbs values. A state is thermal at $ \beta $ if all two-time correlation functions of its observables satisfy the KMS condition, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kms}, with that $ \beta $: $ C^>_{AB}(\omega) = e^{\beta\omega} C^<_{AB}(\omega) $ for all $ A $ and $ B $. Equivalently, the fluctuation--dissipation ratio of Sec.~\ref{sec:gen-fdt} gives the same $ \beta $ at all frequencies and for all observables. For infinite systems, Haag, Hugenholtz and Winnink showed that the KMS condition characterizes the equilibrium states~\cite{HHW1967}. It is tied to thermodynamics by two further results. Stability under local perturbations singles out the KMS states~\cite{HKTP1974}. A state from which no work can be extracted by any cyclic change of the Hamiltonian, even when many copies are available (complete passivity), is a Gibbs state or a ground state~\cite{PuszWoronowicz1978,Lenard1978}.
\end{enumerate}
In a finite system these criteria can hold only up to finite-size corrections, and for the whole of a closed system they never hold (Sec.~\ref{sec:invariants}).
The analysis below uses \ref{t:fixed} throughout: the temperature is that of the bath in Secs.~\ref{sec:bath} and~\ref{sec:quantum}, and the energy of the initial state fixes it in Sec.~\ref{sec:mz}.
Criteria \ref{t:state} and \ref{t:kms} appear as the Gibbs distribution of Sec.~\ref{sec:averaging}, the KMS condition of Sec.~\ref{sec:kms} and the generalized fluctuation--dissipation relation of Sec.~\ref{sec:gen-fdt}.

\section{Closed Chain of Harmonic Oscillators}
\label{sec:chain}

\subsection{Model and method: normal modes}

Consider $ N $ particles of mass $ m $ on a line, connected by springs of stiffness $ \kappa $, with fixed ends,
\begin{equation}
  H = \sum_{j=1}^{N} \frac{p_j^2}{2m} + \frac{\kappa}{2} \sum_{j=0}^{N} (x_{j+1} - x_j)^2 ,
  \qquad x_0 = x_{N+1} = 0 ,
  \label{eq:chain-H}
\end{equation}
where $ x_j $ is the displacement of particle $ j $ from its rest position.
The method is the normal-mode transformation, which applies to every Hamiltonian quadratic in coordinates and momenta and is exact~\cite{ThorntonMarion2004}.
Write $ H = \mathbf p^{\mathsf T}\mathbf p/2m + \mathbf x^{\mathsf T} \Phi \, \mathbf x / 2 $ with a real symmetric, positive-definite force-constant matrix $ \Phi $.
An orthogonal matrix $ S $ diagonalizes it, $ S^{\mathsf T} \Phi S = m \, \mathrm{diag}(\omega_k^2) $, and the normal coordinates $ \mathbf Q = S^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x $, $ \mathbf P = S^{\mathsf T}\mathbf p $ are canonical because $ S $ is orthogonal.
For the chain,
\begin{equation}
  S_{jk} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{N+1}} \sin\frac{\pi j k}{N+1} ,
  \qquad
  \omega_k = 2\omega_0 \sin\frac{\pi k}{2(N+1)} ,
  \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{\kappa/m} ,
  \label{eq:chain-modes}
\end{equation}
with $ k = 1, \dots, N $.
The Hamiltonian separates into independent oscillators,
\begin{equation}
  H = \sum_{k=1}^{N} E_k ,
  \qquad
  E_k = \frac{P_k^2}{2m} + \frac{m \omega_k^2 Q_k^2}{2} ,
  \label{eq:chain-Ek}
\end{equation}
and the motion is solved in closed form:
\begin{equation}
  Q_k(t) = Q_k(0) \cos\omega_k t + \frac{P_k(0)}{m\omega_k} \sin\omega_k t ,
  \qquad
  x_j(t) = \sum_k S_{jk} Q_k(t) .
  \label{eq:chain-solution}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Conservation laws and their consequences}

Every normal-mode energy $ E_k $ is a constant of motion, and $ \{ E_k , E_l \} = 0 $.
The chain has $ N $ degrees of freedom and $ N $ independent conserved quantities in involution, so it is integrable (Appendix~\ref{app:integrability}).
In action--angle variables $ I_k = E_k/\omega_k $ and $ \theta_k = \theta_k(0) + \omega_k t $, each trajectory winds on the torus of fixed $ \{I_k\} $.
Three consequences follow.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.2em]
\item The distribution of energy among the modes is frozen. In the Gibbs state the $ E_k $ are independent and exponentially distributed with mean $ T $ (equipartition). An initial state with the energy in a few modes keeps it there for all times.
\item For an ensemble of initial conditions, the joint distribution of the actions, $ P(I_1,\dots,I_N) $, is conserved exactly, while the angles dephase. Long-time averages are therefore given by the initial action distribution with uniformly distributed angles, i.e.\ by a GGE, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gge}, with $ I_k = E_k $. It is the Gibbs state only if the initial action distribution already was thermal.
\item Local observables equilibrate by dephasing, but to values fixed by the initial mode energies. For non-degenerate frequencies the time average of the momentum variance of particle $ j $ is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\overline{p_j^2}}{m} = \sum_k S_{jk}^2 \, E_k ,
  \label{eq:chain-local}
\end{equation}
a weighted average of the mode energies. It defines a site-dependent kinetic temperature that equals a common $ T $ only if the mode energies are equal.
\end{enumerate}
For finite $ N $ the motion is quasi-periodic, so correlation functions recur.
The harmonic chain therefore cannot thermalize by itself: neither the whole nor any reduced description approaches a Gibbs state unless the initial state is thermal to begin with.
The same holds for its continuum limit, the wave equation, whose Fourier components are the normal modes.

\subsection{Numerical illustration}
\label{sec:chain-numerics}

The illustration follows the numerical experiment of Fermi, Pasta, Ulam and Tsingou~\cite{FPU1955}.
A chain of $ N = 32 $ particles receives an energy density $ \varepsilon = E/N = 0.1\,\kappa a^2 $ (with $ a $ the lattice constant) in its four lowest modes, with random phases.
Two diagnostics are used.
The first is the effective fraction of excited modes,
\begin{equation}
  n_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{1}{N} \exp\Big( - \sum_k w_k \ln w_k \Big) ,
  \qquad w_k = \frac{E_k}{\sum_{k'} E_{k'}} ,
  \label{eq:neff}
\end{equation}
which equals $ 4/32 = 0.125 $ initially and $ e^{\gamma_{\mathrm{E}} - 1} \approx 0.655 $ at equipartition\footnote{At equipartition the $ E_k $ are independent and exponentially distributed; then $ \sum_k w_k \ln w_k \to 1 - \gamma_{\mathrm{E}} - \ln N $ for large $ N $, with $ \gamma_{\mathrm{E}} $ Euler's constant.}.
The second is the nearest-neighbour momentum correlation $ c_1 = \sum_j p_j p_{j+1} / \sum_j p_j^2 $, which vanishes in the Gibbs state, where different momenta are independent, and is close to $ 1 $ for long-wavelength motion.
Both stay at their initial values, and the mode spectrum is frozen (Fig.~\ref{fig:chain}): modes $ k > 4 $ never receive energy.
Appendix~\ref{app:fput} shows how an anharmonic coupling of the modes changes this.

\section{Harmonic System Weakly Coupled to a Large Bath}
\label{sec:bath}

\subsection{Model}

The system consists of a few coupled harmonic oscillators, with unit masses and mass-weighted coordinates $ x_i $,
\begin{equation}
  H_S = \sum_i \frac{p_i^2}{2} + \frac12 \mathbf x^{\mathsf T} K_S \mathbf x ,
  \qquad
  K_S = U \, \mathrm{diag}(\Omega_a^2) \, U^{\mathsf T} ,
  \label{eq:HS}
\end{equation}
with normal-mode frequencies $ \Omega_a $ and normal coordinates $ Q_a = \sum_i U_{ia} x_i $.
It couples through the coordinate $ x_1 $ to a bath of $ N_B $ harmonic oscillators $ (q_k, p_k) $, written in the Caldeira--Leggett form~\cite{Caldeira1983,Zwanzig1973}
\begin{equation}
  H_B = \sum_{k=1}^{N_B} \bigg[ \frac{p_k^2}{2} + \frac{\omega_k^2}{2} \Big( q_k - \frac{c_k}{\omega_k^2} x_1 \Big)^2 \bigg]
  = \sum_k \Big[ \frac{p_k^2}{2} + \frac{\omega_k^2 q_k^2}{2} \Big] - x_1 \sum_k c_k q_k + \frac{x_1^2}{2} \sum_k \frac{c_k^2}{\omega_k^2} .
  \label{eq:HB}
\end{equation}
The last term (the counter-term) ensures that $ H = H_S + H_B $ is bounded below and that the bath does not shift the static potential of the system.
The bath enters all results only through its spectral density
\begin{equation}
  J(\omega) = \frac{\pi}{2} \sum_k \frac{c_k^2}{\omega_k} \, \delta(\omega - \omega_k) ,
  \label{eq:Jclass}
\end{equation}
which combines the coupling strengths with the density of bath frequencies.
The example used for the numerics is Ohmic, $ J(\omega) = \eta \, \omega $ for $ \omega < \omega_c $; the constant $ \eta $ has units of frequency and is the friction coefficient.

The whole, system plus bath, is a closed harmonic system of the kind treated in Sec.~\ref{sec:chain}.
It does not thermalize as a whole.
The question is whether the system alone does.

\subsection{Exact elimination of the bath}
\label{sec:gle}

The bath equations, $ \ddot q_k = -\omega_k^2 q_k + c_k x_1 $, are linear with a given source $ x_1(t) $, and are solved exactly.
This is the first step of the method and involves no approximation.
Inserting the solution into the force $ \sum_k c_k q_k - x_1 \sum_k c_k^2/\omega_k^2 $ exerted by the bath on $ x_1 $, and integrating by parts once, gives the generalized Langevin equation (GLE)
\begin{equation}
  \ddot x_1(t) = -\frac{\partial V_S}{\partial x_1} - \int_0^t \dd s \, \gamma(t-s) \, \dot x_1(s) + \xi(t) ,
  \label{eq:gle-class}
\end{equation}
with $ V_S = \mathbf x^{\mathsf T} K_S \mathbf x/2 $, the friction kernel and the noise
\begin{align}
  \gamma(t) &= \sum_k \frac{c_k^2}{\omega_k^2} \cos\omega_k t = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \dd\omega \, \frac{J(\omega)}{\omega} \cos\omega t ,
  \label{eq:friction}\\
  \xi(t) &= \sum_k c_k \Big[ \Big( q_k(0) - \frac{c_k}{\omega_k^2} x_1(0) \Big) \cos\omega_k t + \frac{p_k(0)}{\omega_k} \sin\omega_k t \Big] .
  \label{eq:noise}
\end{align}
The other system coordinates obey their Hamiltonian equations unchanged.
If the bath is initially in the Gibbs state at temperature $ T $ for the given $ x_1(0) $, the $ q_k(0) - c_k x_1(0)/\omega_k^2 $ and $ p_k(0) $ are independent Gaussian variables with variances $ T/\omega_k^2 $ and $ T $, and
\begin{equation}
  \langle \xi(t) \rangle = 0 ,
  \qquad
  \langle \xi(t) \, \xi(s) \rangle = T \, \gamma(t-s) .
  \label{eq:fdt-class}
\end{equation}
The noise is Gaussian, and its correlation function is proportional to the friction kernel.
This is the fluctuation--dissipation theorem of the second kind: friction and fluctuations have the same microscopic origin.
For an Ohmic bath with $ \omega_c \to \infty $, $ \gamma(t) = 2\eta\,\delta(t) $ and Eq.~\eqref{eq:gle-class} becomes the ordinary Langevin equation with friction $ \eta $.
Equation~\eqref{eq:gle-class} is an instance of the projection-operator equations of Sec.~\ref{sec:mz-method}; for a harmonic bath the projection can be carried out in closed form~\cite{Zwanzig1973,Zwanzig2001}.

\subsection{Weak-coupling perturbation theory}
\label{sec:averaging}

The GLE is exact but still contains the full memory of the bath.
The second step is perturbation theory in the coupling, organized by the separation of time scales it creates.
The small parameter is the ratio of the relaxation rate $ \Gamma $ (defined below) to the system frequencies $ \Omega_a $ and to their differences $ |\Omega_a - \Omega_b| $.

Introduce the complex amplitude of normal mode $ a $,
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_a = \frac{\Omega_a Q_a + \ii P_a}{\sqrt 2} ,
  \qquad
  E_a = |\alpha_a|^2 ,
  \label{eq:amplitude}
\end{equation}
where $ P_a = \dot Q_a $ and $ E_a $ is the energy of the mode.
Since the bath force acts on $ x_1 = \sum_a u_a Q_a $, with $ u_a \equiv U_{1a} $, the exact equation of motion is $ \dot\alpha_a = -\ii\Omega_a \alpha_a + \ii u_a F(t)/\sqrt 2 $, with $ F $ the sum of the memory and noise terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gle-class}.
The slowly varying amplitude $ a_a = \alpha_a e^{\ii\Omega_a t} $ changes only through $ F $.
Its equation is averaged over an interval $ \Delta t $ that is long compared with the bath correlation time $ \tau_B $ (the decay time of $ \gamma $), with $ 1/\Omega_a $ and with $ 1/|\Omega_a - \Omega_b| $, but short compared with $ 1/\Gamma $.
To second order in the couplings $ c_k $ this requires two steps.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item In the memory term, $ \dot x_1(s) $ is replaced by its unperturbed value, $ \dot x_1(s) = \sum_b u_b P_b(s) $ with $ P_b(s) = -\ii(a_b e^{-\ii\Omega_b s} - a_b^* e^{\ii\Omega_b s})/\sqrt 2 $. After averaging, the terms oscillating at $ \Omega_a \pm \Omega_b $ drop out, except the resonant term $ b = a $ (secular, or rotating-wave, approximation). The upper limit of the memory integral is extended to infinity (Markov approximation), which is justified because $ t \gg \tau_B $.
\item The noise, projected on the rotating frame, $ \eta_a(t) = \ii u_a e^{\ii\Omega_a t} \xi(t)/\sqrt 2 $, is correlated over $ \tau_B \ll \Delta t $ and acts as white noise whose strength is its spectral density at $ \Omega_a $.
\end{itemize}
With $ \hat\gamma(\Omega) = \int_0^\infty \dd\tau \, \gamma(\tau) \, e^{\ii\Omega\tau} $, whose real part is $ \mathrm{Re}\,\hat\gamma(\Omega) = J(\Omega)/\Omega $ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:friction}, the result is the Langevin equation
\begin{equation}
  \dot a_a = -\Big( \frac{\Gamma_a}{2} + \ii\delta_a \Big) a_a + \eta_a(t) ,
  \qquad
  \langle \eta_a(t) \, \eta_b^*(s) \rangle = \delta_{ab} \, \Gamma_a T \, \delta(t-s) ,
  \label{eq:langevin-amp}
\end{equation}
with the energy relaxation rate and the frequency shift
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma_a = u_a^2 \, \frac{J(\Omega_a)}{\Omega_a} ,
  \qquad
  \delta_a = \frac{u_a^2}{2} \, \mathrm{Im}\,\hat\gamma(\Omega_a) .
  \label{eq:gamma-class}
\end{equation}
The rate is second order in the coupling and proportional to the density of bath modes at the system frequency: it is the classical golden rule.
Cross-correlations of the noise between different modes, and the correlation $ \langle \eta_a \eta_a \rangle $, oscillate at $ \Omega_a - \Omega_b $ and $ 2\Omega_a $ and average to zero.

Two results follow from Eq.~\eqref{eq:langevin-amp}.
First, by the It\^o rule $ \dd|a|^2 = a^* \dd a + a \, \dd a^* + |\dd\eta|^2 $, the mean energy obeys $ \dd\langle E_a\rangle/\dd t = -\Gamma_a (\langle E_a\rangle - T) $, so
\begin{equation}
  \langle E_a \rangle(t) = T + \big( E_a(0) - T \big) e^{-\Gamma_a t} .
  \label{eq:Ea-relax}
\end{equation}
Second, writing $ a_a = X + \ii Y $, each of $ X $ and $ Y $ is an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process~\cite{Risken1989}. Its probability density obeys the Fokker--Planck equation
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t P = \frac{\Gamma_a}{2} \big[ \partial_X (X P) + \partial_Y (Y P) \big] + \frac{\Gamma_a T}{4} \big( \partial_X^2 + \partial_Y^2 \big) P ,
  \label{eq:fokker-planck}
\end{equation}
whose unique stationary solution is $ P \propto \exp[-(X^2 + Y^2)/T] = \exp(-E_a/T) $, approached from any initial density.
Because the noises of different modes are independent, the joint stationary density is $ \prod_a e^{-E_a/T} = e^{-H_S/T} $: the system reaches the Gibbs state at the bath temperature, up to corrections of first order in the coupling.
The modes, which could not exchange energy in isolation (Sec.~\ref{sec:chain}), are now equilibrated with each other through the bath.

The derivation used four conditions, each of which has a physical meaning.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(C\arabic*), leftmargin=3.0em]
\item \label{c:weak} Weak coupling: $ \Gamma_a \ll \Omega_a $ and $ \Gamma_a \ll |\Omega_a - \Omega_b| $.
\item \label{c:markov} Short bath memory: $ \tau_B \ll 1/\Gamma_a $, i.e.\ $ J(\omega)/\omega $ smooth on the scale $ \Gamma_a $ around $ \Omega_a $.
\item \label{c:large} Large bath: the spacing $ \delta\omega $ of bath frequencies is small compared with $ \Gamma_a $, and times are short compared with the bath recurrence time $ 2\pi/\delta\omega $. The energy released by the system, spread over many bath modes, changes the bath temperature only by an amount of order $ 1/N_B $.
\item \label{c:thermal} Thermal bath: the bath is prepared in a Gibbs state.
\end{enumerate}
Condition~\ref{c:thermal} fixes the final state: the noise strength $ \Gamma_a T $ is set by the temperature at which the bath was prepared, so every system mode relaxes to the same $ T $.
The bath imposes its temperature on the system.

\subsection{Numerical test}
\label{sec:bath-numerics}

The perturbative results are tested against the exact solution of the full quadratic Hamiltonian.
The full $ (N_B + 2) $-dimensional stiffness matrix is diagonalized, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:chain-solution} is applied (Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}), so the only errors are statistical.
The system consists of two oscillators of frequency $ \omega_0 $ coupled by a spring of relative stiffness $ 0.3 $, which gives $ \Omega_+ = \omega_0 $, $ \Omega_- = 1.265\,\omega_0 $ and $ u_\pm^2 = 1/2 $.
The bath has $ N_B = 2000 $ modes, an Ohmic density with $ \eta = 0.04\,\omega_0 $ and $ \omega_c = 5\,\omega_0 $, and temperature $ T $.
Equation~\eqref{eq:gamma-class} predicts $ \Gamma_\pm = 0.02\,\omega_0 $.
The four conditions hold: $ \Gamma/(\Omega_- - \Omega_+) = 0.08 $, $ \delta\omega/\Gamma = 0.13 $, and the recurrence time $ 2\pi/\delta\omega \approx 2500/\omega_0 $ exceeds the simulated time eightfold.
The mode $ \Omega_+ $ starts with energy $ 4T $ and the mode $ \Omega_- $ with zero energy; the results are averaged over 4000 realizations of the phases and of the bath.

Figure~\ref{fig:bath}(a) shows that both modes relax to $ T $ as predicted by Eq.~\eqref{eq:Ea-relax}, without adjustable parameters; the largest deviation, $ 0.05\,T $, is within the statistical error.
The late-time distribution of $ E_\pm $ is exponential with mean $ 1.02\,T $ and $ 1.00\,T $ [Fig.~\ref{fig:bath}(b)].
For a small bath, $ N_B = 100 $ with $ \delta\omega = 0.05\,\omega_0 > \Gamma $, condition~\ref{c:large} fails: fewer than one bath mode lies within the linewidth of each system mode, the bath spectrum is discrete on the relevant scale, and the energy oscillates between the system and the few nearly resonant bath modes instead of relaxing [Fig.~\ref{fig:bath}(c)]. The size of the bath, through the spacing of its frequencies, decides whether the system relaxes.

\subsection{System and bath together}

The relaxation of the system does not make the whole thermal.
The whole is harmonic: its $ N_B + 2 $ normal-mode energies are conserved, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariants} holds.
The excess energy $ E_a(0) - T $ of a system mode ends up in the few global normal modes whose frequencies lie within $ \Gamma_a $ of $ \Omega_a $, where it stays; each carries a share of order $ \delta\omega/\Gamma_a $, invisible in any local observable.
By Eq.~\eqref{eq:mutual}, the entropy gained by the system is matched by correlations between system and bath.
In the finite bath of Fig.~\ref{fig:bath}(c) these correlations return to the system within the observation time.
A harmonic system cannot thermalize by itself (Sec.~\ref{sec:chain}); coupled weakly to a large bath prepared at temperature $ T $, it thermalizes at $ T $, while the closed whole does not.

\section{Quantum System Weakly Coupled to a Bosonic Bath}
\label{sec:quantum}

This section develops the quantum counterpart of Sec.~\ref{sec:bath}.
It shows that the decoherence, the temperature and the entropy of the system are all determined by the two-point correlation function of the bath, and that the temperature enters only through one analytic property of that function.

\subsection{Setting and assumptions}

The system with Hamiltonian $ \hat H_S $ and eigenstates $ |n\rangle $ couples to a bath of bosonic modes,
\begin{equation}
  \hat H = \hat H_S + \hat H_B + \hat A \otimes \hat X ,
  \qquad
  \hat H_B = \sum_k \omega_k \hat b_k^\dagger \hat b_k ,
  \qquad
  \hat X = \sum_k g_k \big( \hat b_k + \hat b_k^\dagger \big) ,
  \label{eq:H-quantum}
\end{equation}
where $ \hat A $ is a system operator.
The spectral density $ J(\omega) = \pi \sum_k g_k^2 \, \delta(\omega - \omega_k) $ coincides with Eq.~\eqref{eq:Jclass} when the quantum oscillators of Sec.~\ref{sec:bath} are written as $ q_k = (\hat b_k + \hat b_k^\dagger)/\sqrt{2\omega_k} $, since then $ g_k = c_k/\sqrt{2\omega_k} $.

The bath state $ \hat\rho_B $ is assumed to be stationary and quasi-free.
A state is quasi-free\footnote{Quasi-free is the name used in quantum statistical mechanics; ``Gaussian'' is the common synonym, referring to the Gaussian form of the Wigner function. The property is defined relative to a set of ladder operators: here those of the normal modes of $ \hat H_B $. It is preserved by linear (Bogoliubov) transformations of the modes and destroyed by non-linear ones.} if all its correlation functions of ladder operators follow from the two-point functions by Wick's rule.
For free bosons, the stationary quasi-free states are the products over modes of geometric distributions, $ \hat\rho_B = \prod_k (1 - x_k) \, x_k^{\hat b_k^\dagger \hat b_k} $ with $ x_k = n_k/(1+n_k) $ and arbitrary mean occupations $ n_k $.
Equivalently, $ \hat\rho_B \propto \exp(-\sum_k \lambda_k \omega_k \hat b_k^\dagger \hat b_k) $: a GGE, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gge}, with one multiplier per mode.
It is also the state of maximum entropy for given $ n_k $\footnote{This is Jaynes's maximum-entropy characterization~\cite{Jaynes1957}: maximizing $ -\Tr\hat\rho\ln\hat\rho $ at fixed mean occupations gives the product of geometric distributions; at fixed mean energy it gives $ e^{-\beta \hat H_B}/Z $, with $ \beta $ the Lagrange multiplier. The principle selects a state; it does not explain why a physical bath is found in it.}.
The Gibbs state is the special case $ \lambda_k = \beta $ for all $ k $, i.e.\ $ n_k = n_{\mathrm B}(\omega_k) = 1/(e^{\beta\omega_k} - 1) $.
A mode in a number (Fock) state is not quasi-free.
For a large bath in which no single mode dominates $ \hat X $ ($ g_k^2 = O(1/N_B) $), $ \hat X $ is a sum of many independent contributions and has Gaussian statistics by the central limit theorem, even without the quasi-free assumption.

Two further assumptions, the same as in Sec.~\ref{sec:bath}, control the analysis:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item the bath is large, so that its spectrum is continuous on the scale of the relaxation rates and the change of each mode caused by the system is of order $ 1/N_B $;
\item the coupling is weak compared with the bandwidth of the bath and with the spacings of the Bohr frequencies $ \omega_{nm} = E_n - E_m $ of the system.
\end{itemize}
Neither assumption requires the bath to be thermal.

Two methods are used, each where it is exact or controlled.
For a coupling that commutes with $ \hat H_S $, the dynamics is solved exactly with the Wick identity (Sec.~\ref{sec:dephasing}).
For a general coupling, the reduced dynamics is obtained to second order in the coupling (Born--Markov--secular approximation, Sec.~\ref{sec:master}), which becomes exact in the weak-coupling limit~\cite{Davies1974}.

\subsection{Wick identity}

For an operator $ \hat X $ linear in the ladder operators and a quasi-free state with $ \langle\hat X\rangle = 0 $,
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle e^{\ii\hat X} \big\rangle = \exp\Big( -\frac12 \langle \hat X^2 \rangle \Big) .
  \label{eq:wick}
\end{equation}
Wick's rule gives $ \langle \hat X^{2k} \rangle = (2k-1)!! \, \langle \hat X^2 \rangle^k $: there are $ (2k-1)!! $ ways to pair $ 2k $ factors, and all pairings give the same value because $ \hat X $ commutes with itself.
Summing the exponential series, $ \sum_k (-1)^k (2k-1)!! \langle\hat X^2\rangle^k/(2k)! = \sum_k (-\langle\hat X^2\rangle/2)^k/k! $.
Equation~\eqref{eq:wick} is the operator form of the statement that the average of an exponential is the exponential of the connected (cumulant) average, and that for Gaussian statistics only the second cumulant survives.
It requires neither a temperature nor a Gibbs state; for the vacuum, $ \langle\hat X^2\rangle $ is the zero-point fluctuation.

\subsection{Decoherence without energy exchange; the Debye--Waller factor}
\label{sec:dephasing}

Let the coupling commute with $ \hat H_S $, $ \hat A |\mu\rangle = a_\mu |\mu\rangle $ (pure dephasing).
Each system state $ |\mu\rangle $ then drives the bath with its own Hamiltonian $ \hat H_B + a_\mu \hat X $ and evolution operator $ \hat U_\mu(t) $, and the reduced density matrix is exactly
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mu\nu}(t) = \rho_{\mu\nu}(0) \, e^{-\ii(E_\mu - E_\nu)t} \, \big\langle \hat U_\nu^\dagger(t) \hat U_\mu(t) \big\rangle_B ,
  \label{eq:dephasing}
\end{equation}
where the partial trace over the bath has become an ordinary bath expectation value by cyclicity of the trace.
Since $ \hat X $ is linear in the modes, $ \hat U_\nu^\dagger \hat U_\mu $ is, up to a phase, a displacement operator: the exponential of a linear combination of ladder operators.
Equation~\eqref{eq:wick} then gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  \big| \big\langle \hat U_\nu^\dagger(t) \hat U_\mu(t) \big\rangle \big|
  = \exp\big[ -(a_\mu - a_\nu)^2 \, \Phi(t) \big] ,
  \\
  \Phi(t) = \int_0^\infty \frac{\dd\omega}{\pi} \, J(\omega) \, \big[ 2n(\omega) + 1 \big] \, \frac{1 - \cos\omega t}{\omega^2} ,
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:decoherence}
\end{equation}
the decoherence function of the independent-boson model~\cite{Mahan2000,Breuer2002}, with $ n(\omega) $ the mode occupation ($ 2n+1 = \coth(\beta\omega/2) $ for a thermal bath).
The populations $ \rho_{\mu\mu} $ do not change: no energy is exchanged, yet the coherences decay.
For an Ohmic bath at $ T > 0 $, $ \Phi(t) \simeq \eta T t $ at long times, and coherences decay exponentially.
For a super-Ohmic bath at $ T = 0 $, $ \Phi(\infty) $ is finite and coherence saturates at a non-zero plateau.

The Debye--Waller factor is the instantaneous version of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dephasing}.
A probe (a neutron, an X-ray photon or a $ \gamma $-ray) that transfers momentum $ \mathbf q $ to a nucleus with displacement $ \hat{\mathbf u} $ acts on the lattice with $ \hat U_{\mathbf q} = e^{\ii \mathbf q \cdot \hat{\mathbf u}} $.
For a probe in a superposition of two momentum transfers, Eq.~\eqref{eq:wick} gives
\begin{equation}
  \langle \hat U_{\mathbf q_2}^\dagger \hat U_{\mathbf q_1} \rangle = e^{-W(\mathbf q_1 - \mathbf q_2)} ,
  \qquad
  W(\mathbf q) = \tfrac12 \big\langle (\mathbf q \cdot \hat{\mathbf u})^2 \big\rangle .
  \label{eq:debye-waller}
\end{equation}
The Debye--Waller exponent is thus the decoherence exponent of the probe; the attenuation of Bragg intensities, $ e^{-2W} $, is its measurable form.
For equal weights of the two branches, the reduced density matrix of the probe has eigenvalues $ \lambda_\pm = (1 \pm e^{-W})/2 $, and its von Neumann entropy
\begin{equation}
  S_{\mathrm{ent}} = -\lambda_+ \ln\lambda_+ - \lambda_- \ln\lambda_-
  \label{eq:S-ent}
\end{equation}
is non-zero already at $ T = 0 $, where $ W $ is set by the zero-point motion.
If the global state is pure, as for a lattice in its ground state, $ S_{\mathrm{ent}} $ is the entanglement entropy between probe and lattice.
The entropy of the probe originates in entanglement with the bath; a temperature only increases it.

\subsection{Temperature as the detailed-balance parameter of the bath}
\label{sec:kms}

Temperature enters through the bath correlation functions
\begin{equation}
  C^>(t) = \langle \hat X(t) \hat X(0) \rangle_B ,
  \qquad
  C^<(t) = \langle \hat X(0) \hat X(t) \rangle_B ,
  \qquad
  C^\gtrless(\omega) = \int \dd t \, e^{\ii\omega t} C^\gtrless(t) ,
  \label{eq:bath-corr}
\end{equation}
with $ \hat X(t) = e^{\ii\hat H_B t} \hat X e^{-\ii\hat H_B t} $.
$ C^>(\omega) $ is the spectral density of transitions in which the bath absorbs energy $ \omega $, and $ C^<(\omega) = C^>(-\omega) $ that of transitions in which it emits $ \omega $.
For the Gibbs state, cyclicity of the trace gives the Kubo--Martin--Schwinger (KMS) condition
\begin{equation}
  C^>(t) = C^<(t + \ii\beta)
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  C^>(\omega) = e^{\beta\omega} \, C^<(\omega) ,
  \label{eq:kms}
\end{equation}
the second form being detailed balance.
With the symmetrized fluctuation $ S_X = (C^> + C^<)/2 $ and the dissipative response $ \chi_X'' = (C^> - C^<)/2 $ (positive for $ \omega > 0 $), Eq.~\eqref{eq:kms} is equivalent to the fluctuation--dissipation theorem
\begin{equation}
  S_X(\omega) = \coth\frac{\beta\omega}{2} \; \chi_X''(\omega) ,
  \qquad
  \coth\frac{\beta\omega}{2} = 1 + 2 n_{\mathrm B}(\omega) .
  \label{eq:fdt}
\end{equation}
For the free bath, $ C^>(\omega) = 2J(\omega)[1 + n(\omega)] $ and $ C^<(\omega) = 2J(\omega)\, n(\omega) $ for $ \omega > 0 $, so the ratio $ C^>/C^< = (1+n)/n $ equals $ e^{\beta\omega} $ at every frequency only if $ n(\omega) = n_{\mathrm B}(\omega) $.
Temperature is therefore a property of the bath correlation function, the parameter of its detailed-balance relation.
In the Debye--Waller exponent, $ W = \frac12 \int \frac{\dd\omega}{2\pi} S_X(\omega) $, it appears only through the factor $ \coth(\beta\omega/2) $.

\subsection{Relaxation: golden-rule rates and the Gibbs state}
\label{sec:master}

For a coupling that does not commute with $ \hat H_S $, the method is second-order perturbation theory for the reduced density matrix $ \hat\rho_S = \Tr_B \hat\rho $.
In the interaction picture, integrating the von Neumann equation twice and tracing over the bath gives, since $ \langle\hat X\rangle = 0 $,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\tilde\rho_S}{\dd t} = -\int_0^t \dd s \, \Tr_B \big[ \tilde V(t), [\tilde V(s), \tilde\rho(s)] \big] .
  \label{eq:born-exact}
\end{equation}
Three approximations, each tied to one of the assumptions above, reduce it to a closed equation.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item Born: $ \tilde\rho(s) \simeq \tilde\rho_S(s) \otimes \hat\rho_B $. The bath is large, so the system changes each bath mode only at order $ 1/N_B $.
\item Markov: $ \tilde\rho_S(s) \to \tilde\rho_S(t) $ and $ \int_0^t \to \int_0^\infty $. The bath correlation time $ \tau_B $ is short compared with the relaxation time.
\item Secular: terms oscillating at differences of Bohr frequencies are dropped. The relaxation rates are small compared with those differences.
\end{itemize}
For a non-degenerate spectrum the populations $ P_n = \langle n | \hat\rho_S | n \rangle $ then obey the Pauli master equation~\cite{Pauli1928,vanHove1955,Breuer2002}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd P_n}{\dd t} = \sum_m \big[ W_{nm} P_m - W_{mn} P_n \big] ,
  \qquad
  W_{mn} = |A_{mn}|^2 \, C^>(E_n - E_m) ,
  \label{eq:pauli}
\end{equation}
where $ W_{mn} $ is the golden-rule rate from $ |n\rangle $ to $ |m\rangle $: the squared matrix element times the ability of the bath to absorb the energy $ E_n - E_m $.
The coherences decay at the rates $ (\Gamma_n + \Gamma_m)/2 $, with $ \Gamma_n = \sum_{m\neq n} W_{mn} $, plus the pure-dephasing contribution of Sec.~\ref{sec:dephasing}.
Davies proved that these equations become exact in the weak-coupling limit, in which the coupling $ \lambda \to 0 $ at fixed $ \lambda^2 t $~\cite{Davies1974}.

Detailed balance of the bath carries over to the rates.
With $ \omega = E_n - E_m $, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kms} gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{W_{mn}}{W_{nm}} = \frac{C^>(\omega)}{C^>(-\omega)} = \frac{C^>(\omega)}{C^<(\omega)} = e^{\beta (E_n - E_m)} ,
  \label{eq:detailed-balance}
\end{equation}
so $ \pi_n = e^{-\beta E_n}/Z_S $ is a stationary solution with every pair of transitions balanced, and it is the only one if the rates connect all levels.
Relaxation to it is monotonic: with $ x_n = P_n/\pi_n $ and the symmetric weights $ w_{nm} = W_{nm}\pi_m $,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t} D(P \Vert \pi) = -\frac12 \sum_{n,m} w_{nm} \, (x_m - x_n)(\ln x_m - \ln x_n) \le 0 ,
  \label{eq:h-theorem}
\end{equation}
the $ H $-theorem of the master equation; Spohn's theorem extends it to the full quantum dynamical semigroup~\cite{Spohn1978}.
The irreversibility enters through the Born and Markov steps, which discard the system--bath correlations, and through the initial product state, which fixes the direction of time.
The structure is that of Boltzmann's kinetic theory, with the golden-rule rates as collision integral and the Born factorization as the counterpart of the molecular-chaos assumption.

For a harmonic oscillator, $ \hat A = (\hat a + \hat a^\dagger)/\sqrt{2\Omega} $, Eq.~\eqref{eq:pauli} gives $ \dd\langle \hat a^\dagger \hat a\rangle/\dd t = -[J(\Omega)/\Omega] \, (\langle \hat a^\dagger\hat a\rangle - n(\Omega)) $: the rate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gamma-class} with $ u_a = 1 $, and the classical result in the limit $ n \to T/\Omega $.

\subsection{Thermality test: generalized fluctuation--dissipation relations}
\label{sec:gen-fdt}

The stationary populations are only one test of thermality.
A stricter test uses the correlation functions of any system observable $ \hat O $ in the stationary state and defines a frequency-dependent inverse temperature by
\begin{equation}
  S_O(\omega) \equiv \coth\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega)\,\omega}{2} \; \chi_O''(\omega) ,
  \label{eq:beta-eff}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ by the measured ratio of fluctuation to dissipation at each frequency.
Equation~\eqref{eq:beta-eff} is a definition; the system is thermal in the operational sense if $ \beta_{\mathrm{eff}} $ is the same constant for all frequencies and all observables.
In the weak-coupling limit with a thermal bath this holds: the generator of the Davies limit satisfies quantum detailed balance with respect to the Gibbs state, and stationary correlation functions computed from it by the quantum regression theorem obey the KMS relation with the bath's $ \beta $~\cite{Davies1974,Breuer2002}.
The criterion fails, informatively, in two situations.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=2.2em]
\item Strong coupling. The reduced equilibrium state is $ \Tr_B e^{-\beta\hat H}/Z \equiv e^{-\beta \hat H_S^*}/Z^* $, which defines the Hamiltonian of mean force $ \hat H_S^* $; it differs from $ \hat H_S $ at first order in the coupling and depends on $ \beta $. The system is thermal with respect to $ \hat H_S^* $, not $ \hat H_S $.
\item Glassy or driven systems. The ratio defines different effective temperatures on different time scales~\cite{Cugliandolo2011}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Fermionic bath}
\label{sec:fermionic}

Three differences arise when the bath is a Fermi sea of conduction electrons.
First, physical couplings contain an even number of fermion operators (fermion-parity superselection\footnote{Superselection rule: no physical observable connects states of even and odd fermion number, so every term of a Hamiltonian contains an even number of fermion operators.}), so the lowest coupling is a bilinear, $ \hat X = \sum_{kk'} g_{kk'} \hat c_k^\dagger \hat c_{k'} $. The bath excitations are then particle--hole pairs, whose density of states at low energy transfer $ \omega $ grows as $ \omega $: the bath is Ohmic.
Second, the KMS condition~\eqref{eq:kms} holds for the even operator $ \hat X $, so the Gibbs state is reached as before, but the rates contain the Pauli factor $ f(\epsilon)[1 - f(\epsilon + \omega)] $. For level splittings $ \omega \ll T $ this gives the Korringa law $ 1/T_1 \propto (g \nu_F)^2 \, T $, with $ \nu_F $ the density of states at the Fermi energy~\cite{Korringa1950}.
Third, Eq.~\eqref{eq:wick} does not apply, because $ \hat X $ is quadratic in the fermion operators. The overlap between the Fermi sea and the Fermi sea in the presence of a local potential with phase shift $ \delta $ vanishes as $ N^{-(\delta/\pi)^2} $ (Anderson's orthogonality catastrophe~\cite{Anderson1967}), and at $ T = 0 $ coherence decays as $ t^{-(\delta/\pi)^2} $~\cite{Nozieres1969}. No coherence plateau analogous to the Debye--Waller factor exists for a Fermi sea.

\subsection{Quantitative example: Debye phonon bath}
\label{sec:debye}

For a Debye model, with density of states $ g(\omega) = 9N\omega^2/\omega_D^3 $ below the Debye frequency $ \omega_D = \Theta_D $, the isotropic exponent of Eq.~\eqref{eq:debye-waller} for a nucleus of mass $ M $ is
\begin{equation}
  W(\mathbf q, T) = \frac{3 q^2}{4 M \omega_D} \, \Phi(T/\Theta_D) ,
  \qquad
  \Phi(x) = \int_0^1 \dd u \, u \coth\frac{u}{2x} ,
  \label{eq:W-debye}
\end{equation}
with the limits
\begin{equation}
  W \simeq
  \begin{cases}
    W_0 \Big[ 1 + \dfrac{2\pi^2}{3} \Big( \dfrac{T}{\Theta_D} \Big)^2 \Big] , & T \ll \Theta_D , \\[8pt]
    4 W_0 \, T/\Theta_D , & T \gg \Theta_D ,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  W_0 = \frac{3 q^2}{8 M \omega_D} .
  \label{eq:W-limits}
\end{equation}
The entanglement entropy~\eqref{eq:S-ent} with $ W = W(\mathbf q_1 - \mathbf q_2, T) $ behaves as $ (W_0/2)[1 - \ln(W_0/2)] $ for $ W_0 \ll 1 $ at $ T = 0 $, and approaches $ \ln 2 - e^{-2W}/2 $ at high temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:debye}).
The thermodynamic entropy of the lattice, $ S_{\mathrm{th}} = \int \dd\omega \, g(\omega)[(1+n_{\mathrm B})\ln(1+n_{\mathrm B}) - n_{\mathrm B}\ln n_{\mathrm B}] $, grows as $ (4\pi^4/5) N (T/\Theta_D)^3 $ at low and as $ 3N[\ln(T/\Theta_D) + 4/3] $ at high temperature.
The two entropies are different quantities.
$ S_{\mathrm{ent}} $, the entropy of a two-branch probe, is bounded by $ \ln 2 $; $ S_{\mathrm{th}} $ is extensive.
What they share is the phonon spectrum $ g(\omega) $ weighted by the occupations.
A measurement of $ W(T) $, through Bragg or M\"ossbauer intensities or interferometric visibility, determines $ g(\omega) $, which in turn predicts $ S_{\mathrm{th}}(T) $; this is a consistency test linking single-probe decoherence to bulk thermodynamics.

(b) Entanglement entropy of a probe in an equal-weight superposition of two momentum transfers, Eq.~\eqref{eq:S-ent}, for three values of the zero-point exponent $ W_0 $.}

\subsection{Scope}

The construction takes the state of the bath as given.
It shows that a weakly coupled system inherits the temperature of that state: if the bath obeys detailed balance at $ \beta $, the system relaxes to its Gibbs state at $ \beta $, with its thermodynamic entropy.
Two questions are left open.
First, why a physical bath is found in a thermal state: canonical typicality~\cite{Goldstein2006,Popescu2006} and the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis~\cite{Deutsch1991,Srednicki1994,Rigol2008,DAlessio2016} (Appendix~\ref{app:eth}) address this for closed systems, and Sec.~\ref{sec:mz} examines a closed system in which the bath is internal.
Second, what happens to the whole: the global state remains pure if it started pure, and the system entropy is matched, by Eq.~\eqref{eq:mutual}, by system--bath correlations.

\section{Slow and Fast Modes in an Isolated Bose Gas: Mori--Zwanzig Analysis}
\label{sec:mz}

\subsection{The Mori--Zwanzig projection method}
\label{sec:mz-method}

In Secs.~\ref{sec:bath} and~\ref{sec:quantum} the system and the bath were given.
In a closed system without an external bath, one must first decide which variables are the ``system''.
The projection-operator method of Mori and Zwanzig~\cite{Nakajima1958,Zwanzig1960,Zwanzig1961,Mori1965} makes this decision explicit.
One chooses a set of relevant variables, and the method rewrites the exact equations of motion for them as a generalized Langevin equation, in which all other degrees of freedom of the same closed system act as a bath through a memory kernel and a fluctuating force.
The rewriting is exact; irreversibility appears only when the memory kernel decays, and a simple (Markovian) description only when it decays fast.
The presentation follows Refs.~\cite{Forster1975,Zwanzig2001,Grabert1982}.

\paragraph{Liouvillian.}
The time evolution of an observable $ A $ is generated by the Liouville operator $ \LL $,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd A}{\dd t} = \ii \LL A ,
  \qquad
  A(t) = e^{\ii\LL t} A ,
  \qquad
  \ii\LL A =
  \begin{cases}
    \{ A , H \} & \text{(classical)} , \\
    \ii [ \hat H , \hat A ] & \text{(quantum)} .
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:liouvillian}
\end{equation}
$ \LL $ acts on the linear space of observables, just as $ \hat H $ acts on the space of state vectors.

\paragraph{Scalar product.}
Observables are treated as vectors with the Kubo scalar product in the equilibrium state $ \hat\rho_{\mathrm{eq}} = e^{-\beta\hat H}/Z $,
\begin{equation}
  \sprod{A}{B} = \frac{1}{\beta} \int_0^\beta \dd\lambda \, \big\langle e^{\lambda\hat H} \hat A^\dagger e^{-\lambda\hat H} \hat B \big\rangle_{\mathrm{eq}} - \langle \hat A^\dagger \rangle_{\mathrm{eq}} \langle \hat B \rangle_{\mathrm{eq}} ,
  \label{eq:kubo}
\end{equation}
which reduces to the covariance $ \langle \delta A^* \, \delta B \rangle_{\mathrm{eq}} $ in classical mechanics.
It is positive, $ \sprod{A}{A} \ge 0 $, and $ \LL $ is Hermitian with respect to it, $ \sprod{A}{\LL B} = \sprod{\LL A}{B} $; both follow from cyclicity of the trace and $ [\hat\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}, \hat H] = 0 $ (classically, from integration by parts).
Hence $ e^{\ii\LL t} $ is unitary.
The Kubo product is chosen because of its physical meaning.
If a static field $ h $ conjugate to $ \hat A $ acts until $ t = 0 $ ($ \hat H \to \hat H - h\hat A $) and is then switched off, linear-response theory gives exactly~\cite{Kubo1957}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\delta\langle \hat A \rangle(t)}{\delta\langle \hat A \rangle(0)} = \frac{C(t)}{C(0)} ,
  \qquad
  C(t) = \sprod{A}{A(t)} ,
  \label{eq:relaxation-function}
\end{equation}
and $ \beta C(0) $ is the static susceptibility.
The correlation function $ C(t) $ is therefore the relaxation of a small non-equilibrium deviation, the quantity that thermalization is about.

\paragraph{Projector.}
For relevant variables $ A_1, \dots, A_n $ with static correlation matrix $ \chi_{ij} = \sprod{A_i}{A_j} $, the operator
\begin{equation}
  \PP X = \sum_{j,l} A_j \, (\chi^{-1})_{jl} \, \sprod{A_l}{X} ,
  \qquad
  \QQ = 1 - \PP ,
  \label{eq:projector}
\end{equation}
is the orthogonal projection onto their linear span.
It satisfies $ \PP^2 = \PP $ and $ \PP A_i = A_i $, and it is Hermitian.
$ \PP X $ is the best linear estimate of $ X $ in terms of the $ A_i $, and $ \QQ X $ is the part of $ X $ uncorrelated with all of them.
The $ \QQ $ space is the bath of the formalism: every degree of freedom not represented by the relevant variables.

\paragraph{Operator identity.}
The derivation rests on the identity
\begin{equation}
  e^{\ii\LL t} = e^{\ii\QQ\LL t} + \int_0^t \dd s \, e^{\ii\LL(t-s)} \, \PP \ii\LL \, e^{\ii\QQ\LL s} ,
  \label{eq:dyson}
\end{equation}
which follows by integrating from $ 0 $ to $ t $ the derivative of $ f(s) = e^{\ii\LL(t-s)} e^{\ii\QQ\LL s} $,
\begin{equation*}
  f'(s) = e^{\ii\LL(t-s)} \big( -\ii\LL + \ii\QQ\LL \big) e^{\ii\QQ\LL s} = -e^{\ii\LL(t-s)} \, \PP \ii\LL \, e^{\ii\QQ\LL s} .
\end{equation*}
It separates the full evolution into the evolution confined to the $ \QQ $ space and the returns to the relevant subspace.

\paragraph{Generalized Langevin equation.}
Split the time derivative as $ \dot A_i(t) = e^{\ii\LL t} (\PP + \QQ) \ii\LL A_i $.
The $ \PP $ part is a linear combination of the $ A_j(t) $.
To the $ \QQ $ part, apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:dyson}.
Because $ \ii\LL $ is anti-Hermitian and $ F_i(s) $ lies in the $ \QQ $ space, $ \sprod{A_l}{\ii\LL F_i(s)} = -\sprod{\QQ\ii\LL A_l}{F_i(s)} = -\sprod{F_l}{F_i(s)} $, which turns the returning term into a memory integral.
The result is exact:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \frac{\dd A_i(t)}{\dd t}
  = \sum_j \ii\Omega_{ij} \, A_j(t)
  - \sum_j \int_0^t \dd s \, K_{ij}(s) \, A_j(t-s)
  + F_i(t)
  }
  \label{eq:gle}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
  \ii\Omega_{ij} &= \sum_l \sprod{A_l}{\ii\LL A_i} (\chi^{-1})_{lj}
  && \text{(frequency matrix)},
  \label{eq:omega}\\
  F_i(t) &= e^{\ii\QQ\LL t} \, \QQ \ii\LL A_i
  && \text{(fluctuating force)},
  \label{eq:force}\\
  K_{ij}(t) &= \sum_l \sprod{F_l}{F_i(t)} (\chi^{-1})_{lj}
  && \text{(memory kernel)}.
  \label{eq:kernel}
\end{align}
The frequency term describes the instantaneous, reversible motion within the relevant subspace.
The fluctuating force is the part of $ \dot A_i $ that cannot be expressed through the relevant variables; it evolves in the $ \QQ $ space and remains uncorrelated with them, $ \sprod{A_j}{F_i(t)} = 0 $ for all $ t $.
The memory kernel is the autocorrelation of the fluctuating force (the fluctuation--dissipation theorem of the second kind~\cite{Kubo1966}), exactly as $ \gamma(t) $ is the noise correlation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fdt-class}.
For the harmonic bath of Sec.~\ref{sec:bath}, the $ \QQ $ space is the bath and Eq.~\eqref{eq:gle} is Eq.~\eqref{eq:gle-class}.

Taking the scalar product of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gle} with $ A_k $ removes the fluctuating force and gives a closed equation for the correlation matrix $ C_{ij}(t) = \sprod{A_j}{A_i(t)} $,
\begin{equation}
  \dot C(t) = \ii\Omega \, C(t) - \int_0^t \dd s \, K(s) \, C(t-s) ,
  \qquad
  \tilde C(z) = \big[ z - \ii\Omega + \tilde K(z) \big]^{-1} \chi ,
  \label{eq:corr-eq}
\end{equation}
with the Laplace transform $ \tilde f(z) = \int_0^\infty \dd t \, e^{-zt} f(t) $.
For a single Hermitian variable of definite time-reversal parity, $ \Omega = 0 $.

\paragraph{Exactness.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:gle} contains exactly the information of the microscopic equations and is time-reversal symmetric, since $ C(-t) = C(t) $ for such a variable.
Nothing has been approximated; in particular, no information has been discarded.
Irreversible decay of $ C(t) $ requires the memory kernel to decay, and the physical question is how a kernel can decay in a closed system that cannot dissipate information.

\paragraph{How a memory kernel decays in a closed system.}
$ K(t) $ is a correlation function of the projected dynamics $ \QQ\LL\QQ $.
In a finite quantum system, or any system with a discrete spectrum, it has the form
\begin{equation}
  K(t) = \sum_{\alpha} w_\alpha \cos \nu_\alpha t ,
  \qquad
  \overline{K^2} = \frac12 \sum_{\alpha} w_\alpha^2 > 0 ,
  \label{eq:kernel-sum}
\end{equation}
with weights $ w_\alpha $ and frequencies $ \nu_\alpha $ given by the eigenvalues of $ \QQ\LL\QQ $, and $ \overline{\cdots} $ the infinite-time average (written for non-degenerate, non-zero frequencies).
Such an almost-periodic function cannot decay for ever: its mean square is non-zero, and it returns arbitrarily close to its initial value.
It can, however, decay for all practical purposes, by dephasing.
Suppose the weight is spread over $ M_{\mathrm{eff}} = (\sum_\alpha w_\alpha)^2 / \sum_\alpha w_\alpha^2 \gg 1 $ frequencies with spread $ \sigma_\nu $, and the frequencies are non-degenerate and incommensurate.
Then the terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:kernel-sum} lose their phase relation after a time $ \tau_c \sim 1/\sigma_\nu $, and $ K(t) $ falls from $ K(0) $ to fluctuations of relative size $ M_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1/2} $.
It stays there until the phases realign, which takes at least the inverse spacing of neighbouring frequencies and, for incommensurate frequencies, a time that grows exponentially with $ M_{\mathrm{eff}} $.
A strictly continuous spectrum is the limit $ M_{\mathrm{eff}} \to \infty $ of this mechanism, not a requirement for it.

No information is lost in the process.
It is moved into phase relations between the relevant variables and a large number of $ \QQ $-space components, which no relevant observable can read.
Reversing all velocities (an echo experiment) would restore it.
The mechanism is the same as the decoherence of Sec.~\ref{sec:dephasing}, where $ \Phi(t) $ grows because the bath frequencies dephase, and as the damping of Sec.~\ref{sec:bath}, where the $ \approx \Gamma_a/\delta\omega $ bath modes within a linewidth play the role of the $ M_{\mathrm{eff}} $ frequencies.
It has two requirements.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item A large $ \QQ $ space. The decisive parameter is the size of the bath, internal or external. The spacing of the frequencies decreases with size: $ \delta\omega \propto 1/N_B $ for a bath of $ N_B $ modes, and the mean level spacing is $ \sim W/D $ for a many-body spectrum of width $ W $ and Hilbert-space dimension $ D $. On times $ t \ll 1/\delta\omega $ the spectrum cannot be distinguished from a continuum, and $ M_{\mathrm{eff}} $ grows with the size. A small system has well-separated levels, few effective frequencies, and a kernel that returns quickly.
\item Non-degenerate, incommensurate frequencies. In non-integrable systems level repulsion (Appendix~\ref{app:integrability}) removes degeneracies. In integrable systems the conserved quantities put weight at zero frequency (Mazur's bound, below) and make frequencies commensurate, so the kernel does not decay; the harmonic chain of Sec.~\ref{sec:chain} is the extreme case.
\end{itemize}
In classical mechanics a further route exists: in a mixing\footnote{A dynamical system is mixing if the correlation of any two phase-space functions decays to the product of their averages. Mixing implies ergodicity, but not conversely.} (chaotic) system the Liouvillian has a continuous spectrum even with few degrees of freedom. Correlations of smooth observables decay because phase-space densities are stretched into ever finer filaments, and the information moves to scales that smooth observables do not resolve. Quantum mechanics stops this at the scale of Planck's constant, after which the mechanism above takes over.
In a finite system the residual $ M_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1/2} $ is a finite-size memory of the initial state; Sec.~\ref{sec:lenk-mz} measures it.

\paragraph{Definition of slow and fast variables.}
Suppose $ K(t) $ has decayed after a time $ \tau_c $, the correlation time of the $ \QQ $ space, and the dissipative part of the dynamics of the relevant variables is slow on this scale.
Then $ C(t-s) \simeq C(t) $ over the range of the kernel, up to the reversible oscillation generated by $ \Omega $, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:corr-eq} becomes
\begin{equation}
  C(t) \simeq e^{(\ii\Omega - \Gamma) t} \, \chi ,
  \qquad
  \Gamma = \tilde K(0) = \int_0^\infty \dd t \, K(t) ,
  \label{eq:markov}
\end{equation}
for non-oscillating variables ($ \Omega = 0 $); for oscillating ones, $ \tilde K $ is evaluated at their frequencies.
This Markov limit is the step at which the exact, reversible equation becomes an irreversible one.
$ K(0) = \sprod{\QQ\LL A}{\QQ\LL A}/\sprod{A}{A} $ is the mean-square coupling of $ A $ to the $ \QQ $ space, and $ \Gamma \approx K(0)\,\tau_c $ is coupling squared times correlation time, the structure of the golden rule.
The limit is consistent if
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon \equiv \Gamma \, \tau_c \approx K(0) \, \tau_c^2 \ll 1 .
  \label{eq:markov-condition}
\end{equation}
This condition is the definition used here.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item A set of relevant variables, containing every conserved quantity it overlaps with, is a set of slow variables if its memory kernel decays within a time $ \tau_c $ and $ \varepsilon \ll 1 $.
\item The fast variables are then the $ \QQ $ space of that set, whose correlations dephase within $ \tau_c $.
\end{itemize}
The slow variables are weakly coupled to the fast ones, which act on them as a bath with correlation time $ \tau_c $: they impose relaxation at the rate $ \Gamma $ and decohere any superposition of slow configurations.
A ``mode'' is here a direction in the space of observables, i.e.\ a linear combination of variables, not necessarily a normal mode.

The definition is testable, and it can be applied to a candidate set of $ n $ physically accessible variables without knowing the slow ones beforehand.
Diagonalize the normalized propagator of the set,
\begin{equation}
  T(\tau) = \chi^{-1/2} \, C(\tau) \, \chi^{-1/2} ,
  \qquad
  T(\tau) \simeq e^{-\chi^{-1/2}\Gamma\chi^{1/2} \tau} \quad (\tau \gg \tau_c) .
  \label{eq:propagator}
\end{equation}
For time-reversal-even variables its eigenvalues are real and lie between $ -1 $ and $ 1 $.
If the set splits into slow and fast directions, then in the window $ \tau_c \ll \tau \ll 1/\Gamma $ the eigenvalues form two groups: those of the slow directions stay close to $ 1 $, those of the fast directions are close to $ 0 $.
The gap between the groups in this window is the operational signature of time-scale separation, and the eigenvectors of the first group are the slow modes.
If the eigenvalues instead decay over a continuum of times with no gap, the division into slow and fast is not defined, and only the exactly conserved quantities (eigenvalue $ 1 $ for all $ \tau $) are slow.

In Secs.~\ref{sec:bath} and~\ref{sec:quantum} the system coordinates are slow in this sense: for Fig.~\ref{fig:bath}, $ \Gamma = 0.02\,\omega_0 $ and $ \tau_c \sim 1/\omega_c = 0.2/\omega_0 $, so $ \varepsilon \approx 4\times 10^{-3} $.
Other standard examples are conserved densities at long wavelength, whose relaxation rates vanish as the wavenumber goes to zero (the basis of hydrodynamics~\cite{Forster1975}).

\paragraph{Conserved quantities.}
A relevant variable that overlaps with a conserved quantity $ I $ (e.g.\ the energy) cannot relax completely: its correlation function retains a plateau at least equal to $ \sprod{A}{I}^2/\sprod{I}{I} $ (Mazur's bound~\cite{Mazur1969}), and then $ \tilde K(0) = 0 $.
All conserved quantities that overlap with the relevant variables must therefore be included among them.
For a single variable this amounts to replacing $ A $ by its component orthogonal to $ I $, $ A_\perp = A - I \sprod{I}{A}/\sprod{I}{I} $.

\paragraph{A hierarchy of time scales.}
The division into slow and fast variables is the simplest case of a more general structure~\cite{Bogoliubov1946}.
Characterize each eigenvalue of Eq.~\eqref{eq:propagator}, for a large candidate set, by a rate $ \gamma_k $, e.g.\ the inverse of the lag at which it falls to $ 1/e $, and order them, $ 0 = \gamma_1 = \dots = \gamma_c < \gamma_{c+1} \le \gamma_{c+2} \le \dots $, the first $ c $ belonging to the conserved quantities.
A gap $ \gamma_k \ll \gamma_{k+1} $ at any position defines a partition in the sense above, and several gaps define several tiers, from the fastest variables down to the exactly conserved ones.
Eliminating the fastest tier gives a generalized Langevin equation for the rest; eliminating the next tier from it gives the next, and so on.
Each step resembles one step of a Wilsonian renormalization group~\cite{WilsonKogut1974}, with time scale in place of length or energy scale, and the resemblance has been used to derive slow dynamics systematically~\cite{Chen1996}.
The eliminated tier shifts the frequencies of the remaining variables through the reactive part of $ \tilde K $, adds damping and noise through its dissipative part, and the effects of its finite memory are suppressed by powers of $ \varepsilon $ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:markov-condition}, as irrelevant couplings are suppressed by powers of the ratio of scales.
The end point of the iteration, the analogue of an infrared fixed point, is the set of exactly conserved quantities, and the state it leads to is the Gibbs state, or the GGE~\eqref{eq:gge} if there are conserved quantities besides the energy.
In an extended system, the conserved densities at long wavelength approach this end point continuously, with rates that vanish as the wavenumber goes to zero; hydrodynamics is the effective theory of that regime.

The analogy has a limit.
The tiers are ordered by relaxation rate, not by energy or oscillation frequency.
A variable can oscillate fast and relax slowly: the system oscillators of Sec.~\ref{sec:bath} oscillate at $ \omega_0 $ and relax at $ \Gamma = 0.02\,\omega_0 $.
High-frequency variables that do not relax, but follow the slow ones adiabatically, as electrons follow nuclei in the Born--Oppenheimer approximation, are eliminated in the same way, but contribute only to the reactive part of $ \tilde K $.
This reactive elimination is the close counterpart of integrating out high-energy modes in the renormalization group.
The dissipative part, and with it thermalization, requires in addition that the eliminated tier dephase, as described above.

\paragraph{Where the line is drawn.}
Which tier is the system and which is the bath is therefore not fixed by the Hamiltonian.
It is fixed by the time scale of observation $ t $, and with respect to $ t $ the variables fall into three groups, not two.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item Fast variables, $ \gamma_k t \gg 1 $, have relaxed. They form the bath, and their state is the equilibrium state conditioned on the current values of the slower variables.
\item Dynamical variables, $ \gamma_k t \sim 1 $, are the ones whose relaxation is observed: the system.
\item Frozen variables, $ \gamma_k t \ll 1 $, have not yet changed. They act as additional constants of motion.
\end{itemize}
This is Feynman's definition of equilibrium: ``if all the `fast' things have happened and all the `slow' things not''~\cite{Feynman1972}.
On the scale $ t $, the state reached is the constrained equilibrium state
\begin{equation}
  \hat\rho_t \simeq Z^{-1} \exp\Big( - \beta \hat H - \sum_{k\,\text{frozen}} \lambda_k \hat A_k \Big) ,
  \label{eq:constrained}
\end{equation}
which has the form of the GGE~\eqref{eq:gge}, with the frozen variables in place of exact invariants and their multipliers fixed by their initial values.
As $ t $ grows, variables pass from frozen to dynamical to fast, the multipliers are released tier by tier, and the sequence of constrained states ends in the Gibbs state.
Relaxation to such an intermediate state is known as prethermalization~\cite{Berges2004,Kollar2011,Mori2018}; the frozen variables are its approximate conservation laws.
In the same way the observation time decides which regions of phase space are explored ergodically~\cite{Palmer1982}.

Each constrained state can carry its own temperature.
A system of nuclear spins reaches internal equilibrium within the spin--spin time $ T_2 $, while it exchanges energy with the lattice only within $ T_1 \gg T_2 $; for $ T_2 \ll t \ll T_1 $ the spin energy is frozen, and its multiplier is the spin temperature~\cite{AbragamProctor1958}.
The conduction electrons of a metal heated by a short laser pulse thermalize among themselves within femtoseconds, at a temperature well above that of the lattice, with which they equilibrate within picoseconds~\cite{Anisimov1974}.
In both cases criteria \ref{t:fixed}--\ref{t:kms} hold with respect to Eq.~\eqref{eq:constrained}, not to the Gibbs state of the whole: the several temperatures of Sec.~\ref{sec:thermal-criteria} are the diagnosis of a constrained equilibrium, and they depend on the time scale because the set of frozen variables does.

The choice of relevant variables, and of $ t $, is the observer's.
Whether the choice yields a closed, Markovian description is not: it does so only if the cut lies in a gap, i.e.\ if $ \varepsilon \ll 1 $ with respect to the next faster tier and $ \gamma t \ll 1 $ for the next slower one.
A cut through a continuum of rates leaves variables of comparable speed on both sides, and the reduced dynamics is non-Markovian however the line is drawn.

\paragraph{Density-matrix form.}
The same construction applied to density matrices, with $ \PP\hat\rho = \Tr_B(\hat\rho) \otimes \hat\rho_B $, gives the Nakajima--Zwanzig equation~\cite{Nakajima1958,Zwanzig1960}.
It is an exact equation for $ \hat\rho_S $ with a memory kernel, whose inhomogeneous term vanishes for a product initial state; to second order in the coupling its kernel is the double commutator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:born-exact}.
The master equation of Sec.~\ref{sec:master} is therefore its weak-coupling Markov limit~\cite{Breuer2002}.

\paragraph{Numerical evaluation.}
When the exact $ C(t) $ is available, the memory kernel follows from it.
For a single variable with $ \Omega = 0 $, differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:corr-eq} once and using $ \dot C(0) = 0 $ gives the Volterra equation of the second kind
\begin{equation}
  K(t) \, C(0) = -\ddot C(t) - \int_0^t \dd s \, K(s) \, \dot C(t-s) ,
  \label{eq:volterra}
\end{equation}
which is solved step by step in $ t $.
Comparing the decay of $ K(t) $ with that of $ C(t) $ tests Eq.~\eqref{eq:markov-condition} directly.

\subsection{Model of Lenk et al.}
\label{sec:lenk-model}

Lenk, Biswas, Posazhennikova and Kroha~\cite{Lenk2025} study $ N $ bosons in $ M = 5 $ single-particle levels,
\begin{equation}
  \hat H = \Delta \sum_{i=1}^{M} (i-1) \, \hat n_i
  + J \sum_{i \neq j} \hat b_i^\dagger \hat b_j
  + U \sum_i \hat b_i^\dagger \hat b_i^\dagger \hat b_i \hat b_i
  + U' \!\!\! \sum_{\substack{i,j,l,m \\ \text{not } i=j=l=m}} \!\!\! \hat b_i^\dagger \hat b_j^\dagger \hat b_l \hat b_m ,
  \label{eq:lenk-H}
\end{equation}
with $ \hat n_i = \hat b_i^\dagger \hat b_i $, level spacing $ \Delta/J = 10 $, intra-level interaction $ U/J = 1 $ and inter-level interaction $ U'/J = 0.1 $.
Energies are measured in units of $ J $.
With $ \hat B = \sum_i \hat b_i $ and $ \hat K = \hat B^\dagger \hat B $, and using $ [\hat B, \hat B^\dagger] = M $, the all-to-all terms are
\begin{equation}
  J \sum_{i\neq j} \hat b_i^\dagger \hat b_j = J (\hat K - N) ,
  \qquad
  U' \!\!\! \sum_{\text{not } i=j=l=m} \!\!\! \hat b_i^\dagger \hat b_j^\dagger \hat b_l \hat b_m = U' \Big( \hat K^2 - M\hat K - \sum_i \hat n_i(\hat n_i - 1) \Big) .
  \label{eq:lenk-K}
\end{equation}
Since $ \hat K = M \hat n_0 $, where $ \hat n_0 $ is the occupation of the uniform superposition $ \hat B/\sqrt M $ of all levels, the $ U' $ term is essentially $ U' M^2 \hat n_0^2 $.
Its scale, $ U' N^2 $, is $ 40 $ for $ N = 20 $ and comparable to the total level spread $ \Delta(M-1) = 40 $ and to the intra-level interaction energy.
The model therefore has no small parameter.
The total particle number and the energy are conserved; there is no other symmetry.

The model is chaotic: the mean ratio of consecutive level spacings~\cite{Oganesyan2007} is $ \langle r\rangle = 0.515, 0.523, 0.533, 0.536 $ for $ N = 10, 15, 20, 25 $ (middle 60\% of the spectrum), compared with $ 0.531 $ for the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble and $ 0.386 $ for uncorrelated levels~\cite{Atas2013}.

\subsection{Results of Lenk et al.}
\label{sec:lenk-results}

Lenk \textit{et al.}\ take the occupations of the levels $ i \ge 3 $ as the measured observables.
This divides the Hilbert space into an observed subsystem S (levels 3--5) and an unobserved remainder R (levels 1--2), which they call the bath.
They propagate pure initial states of $ N = 25 $ bosons ($ D = 23\,751 $) with a fourth-order Taylor expansion of the short-time propagator, raised to high powers by repeated multiplication.
Their results are the following.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(R\arabic*), leftmargin=3.0em]
\item \label{r:entropy} Starting from the Fock state $ n_1 = N $ (energy $ U N(N-1) = 600\,J $, broad energy distribution), the entanglement entropy of S rises from $ 0 $ to a stationary value $ \bar S_\infty = 5.15 $, with residual fluctuations of $ 7\times 10^{-3} $. A bi-exponential fit gives $ \tau_1 J = 0.26 \pm 0.01 $ and $ \tau_2 J = 1.58 \pm 0.03 $.
\item \label{r:nsys} The particle number of S, $ n_{\mathrm{sys}} $, approaches its stationary value bi-exponentially as well, with $ \tau_1 J = 0.041 $ and $ \tau_2 J = 2.12 $; the stationary occupations decrease with the level index.
\item \label{r:narrow} An equal-weight superposition of the $ 74 $ eigenstates in the window $ 599.18 < E/J < 600.87 $ (narrow energy distribution, same mean energy) is essentially static; the authors attribute this to the state containing ``essentially only a single frequency''.
\item \label{r:spectra} At the centre-of-mass time $ Jt = 100 $, the ratio of the Keldysh to the spectral function at the five single-particle peaks yields occupations that follow a Bose function with chemical potential $ \mu = 0 $ and a single fitted temperature, $ T/J = 198 \pm 3 $.
\item \label{r:corr} Spectra of occupation correlations between different levels, $ \langle \hat n_i(t_1) \hat n_j(t_2) \rangle $, obey the detailed-balance relation with $ T/J = 193 \pm 17 $ at $ Jt = 1 $ and $ 200 \pm 7 $ at $ Jt = 10 $. Temperatures from different correlators differ by more than the fit errors at intermediate times and agree after averaging over time.
\end{enumerate}
From these results the authors conclude that measurement-induced ``dynamical bath generation'' is a general thermalization mechanism for isolated systems.
They argue that, unlike the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH, Appendix~\ref{app:eth}), it describes the time-dependent approach to equilibrium, applies to broad and narrow energy distributions, and covers correlations that are non-local in level space.
They also state that its only condition is non-integrability, and that the thermodynamic limit is controlled by the Hilbert-space dimension rather than by the particle number.

\subsection{Method}
\label{sec:lenk-method}

The Hamiltonian~\eqref{eq:lenk-H} is diagonalized exactly in the $ N $-particle sector for $ N = 10, 15, 20 $, with Hilbert-space dimensions $ D = \binom{N+4}{4} = 1001, 3876, 10626 $, and for the quench observables also at $ N = 25 $.
Exact diagonalization is used because it gives all correlation functions at all times without truncation or time-step error, and because $ D \le 2.4 \times 10^4 $ is affordable.
The equilibrium state is the canonical state whose mean energy equals that of the initial Fock state; its temperatures are $ T = 14.3, 35.3, 84.1, 207 $ for $ N = 10, 15, 20, 25 $.

Two relevant sets are used.
To identify slow modes without presupposing them, the relaxation spectrum~\eqref{eq:propagator} is computed for a candidate set of eight variables: the occupations $ \hat n_3, \hat n_4, \hat n_5 $, the intra-level interaction energies of S and of R, the uniform-mode occupation $ \hat n_0 $ and its interaction energy $ \hat n_0(\hat n_0 - 1) $, and the level energy $ \sum_i (i-1)\hat n_i $. Their span contains $ \hat H $.
For a detailed analysis, the relevant variable is the particle number of S, $ \hat n_{\mathrm{sys}} = \hat n_3 + \hat n_4 + \hat n_5 $, together with the conserved energy.
Its Kubo relaxation function~\eqref{eq:relaxation-function} is evaluated from the eigenstates, its component along $ \hat H $ is removed, and the memory kernel is obtained from Eq.~\eqref{eq:volterra} (Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}).
For the non-equilibrium dynamics, the initial Fock state is expanded in eigenstates and propagated exactly, and the reduced density matrix of S is computed at each time.

\subsection{Slow and fast variables}
\label{sec:lenk-mz}

\paragraph{Relaxation spectrum.}
Figure~\ref{fig:lenk-mz}(d) shows the eigenvalues of the propagator~\eqref{eq:propagator} of the candidate set.
One eigenvalue equals $ 1 $ at all lags: it belongs to the conserved energy.
The other seven decay from $ 1 $ over a continuum of times, from $ J\tau \approx 0.03 $ to $ J\tau \approx 1 $, and then level off at scattered values between $ 0 $ and $ 0.5 $ that decrease with $ N $ (at $ J\tau = 1 $: $ 0.52 $ to $ -0.04 $ for $ N = 10 $, $ 0.49 $ to $ 0.03 $ for $ N = 15 $).
There is no window in which they split into a group near $ 1 $ and a group near $ 0 $.
By the definition of Sec.~\ref{sec:mz-method}, the only slow variable of the model among these candidates is the energy (together with the particle number, which is fixed); the level occupations and interaction energies are not slow modes.
In the language of the hierarchy of Sec.~\ref{sec:mz-method}, the rates of the candidate variables form a continuum rather than tiers. No cut between them lies in a gap, and the only boundary between tiers is the one between the conserved energy and the rest.

\paragraph{Energy locks half of the occupation fluctuation.}
The Mazur bound with $ \hat I = \hat H $ accounts for $ 58\% $, $ 57\% $ and $ 54\% $ of the equilibrium fluctuation $ \sprod{n_{\mathrm{sys}}}{n_{\mathrm{sys}}} $ for $ N = 10, 15, 20 $.
More than half of a fluctuation of the observed particle number is locked to a fluctuation of the conserved energy and never decays.

\paragraph{Relaxation of the remainder.}
Figure~\ref{fig:lenk-mz}(a) shows the relaxation function of the component of $ \hat n_{\mathrm{sys}} $ orthogonal to $ \hat H $.
It falls within $ Jt \approx 0.3 $ (to $ 0.13 $ for $ N = 20 $), shows a partial revival near $ Jt \approx 1 $, and then decreases slowly to a plateau ($ 0.12 $ at $ Jt = 2 $, $ 0.06 $ at $ Jt = 8 $ for $ N = 20 $).
The initial decay time, $ K(0)^{-1/2} = 0.05/J $ for $ N = 20 $, is set by the mean-square rate of change of $ \hat n_{\mathrm{sys}} $, i.e.\ by the strength of the hopping and pair-scattering processes that move particles between S and R.
The fluctuating force $ \QQ\ii\LL\hat n_{\mathrm{sys}} $ consists of these particle currents, i.e.\ of the one- and two-body coherences $ \hat b_i^\dagger \hat b_j $ and $ \hat b_i^\dagger \hat b_j^\dagger \hat b_l \hat b_m $ that transfer particles between the levels. Their correlations dephase, which is what makes $ C_\perp $ decay: the rest of the closed system acts as an internal bath.

\paragraph{The coupling to the internal bath is not weak.}
The memory kernel [Fig.~\ref{fig:lenk-mz}(b)] has a main lobe of width $ \approx 0.1/J $, followed by an oscillating tail extending to $ Jt \approx 2 $.
Its running integral $ \int_0^t K\,\dd s $ is $ 7.4\,J $ for $ 0.1 < Jt < 0.3 $, falls to $ 1.3\,J $ at $ Jt = 1 $, and keeps drifting ($ 0.9\,J $ at $ Jt = 12 $).
It has no plateau, so no Markov rate $ \Gamma $ exists; with the main-lobe values, $ \varepsilon \approx 7 \times 0.1 \approx 0.7 $, and condition~\eqref{eq:markov-condition} fails.
This is the single-variable counterpart of the missing gap in Fig.~\ref{fig:lenk-mz}(d).
Scaling the off-diagonal processes ($ J $ and $ U' $) down by a factor $ \lambda $ does not open a Markovian window at these sizes. For $ \lambda \lesssim 0.2 $ the level statistics move towards the uncorrelated limit ($ \langle r\rangle = 0.44 $ at $ \lambda = 0.1 $, $ N = 15 $), eigenstates approach Fock states, and the non-decaying plateau grows before $ \varepsilon $ becomes small.

\paragraph{Finite-size memory.}
The plateau of $ C_\perp(t) $ is its infinite-time average, determined by the fluctuations of the eigenstate expectation values $ \langle a | \hat n_{\mathrm{sys}} | a \rangle $ between neighbouring eigenstates.
It decreases as $ D^{-1/2} $ [Fig.~\ref{fig:lenk-mz}(c); fitted exponent $ 0.47 $].
This is the residual $ M_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1/2} $ of the dephasing mechanism of Sec.~\ref{sec:mz-method}, with a number of effectively contributing frequencies proportional to $ D $. It is also the eigenstate-to-eigenstate fluctuation $ e^{-S/2} \propto D^{-1/2} $ predicted by the ETH (Appendix~\ref{app:eth}).
The orthogonal component relaxes completely only for $ D \to \infty $; for finite $ D $ a memory of relative size $ D^{-1/2} $ remains.

\subsection{Relaxation of the observed subsystem after a quench}
\label{sec:lenk-quench}

Figure~\ref{fig:lenk-quench} shows the dynamics from the Fock state $ n_1 = N = 20 $, the non-equilibrium initial state of Ref.~\cite{Lenk2025}.
Two stages are visible.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item Fast stage, $ Jt \lesssim 0.1 $. $ \langle\hat n_1\rangle $ drops from $ 20 $ to $ 11 $, and the Shannon entropy of the Fock-diagonal part of the reduced density matrix of S nearly reaches its final value. The time scale is the inverse energy width of the initial state, $ 1/\sigma_E = 0.023/J $ ($ \sigma_E = 44 $): the Fock state decays into the eigenstates it overlaps with, and the coherences between the Fock configurations it feeds dephase.
\item Slow stage, $ 0.3 \lesssim Jt \lesssim 5 $. The occupations approach their stationary values with damped oscillations, and the von Neumann entropy of S rises to its stationary value. The mean deviation of this entropy from its final value decreases from $ 0.44 $ ($ 0.6 < Jt < 1.2 $) to $ 0.16 $, $ 0.07 $ and $ 0.03 $ in the successive doubled windows, i.e.\ with an effective time constant of $ 1/J $ to $ 2/J $ that grows with time. A single exponential does not describe the stage.
\end{itemize}
The two stages are not the relaxation of two modes, since the relaxation spectrum has no gap; they are the initial dephasing, on the scale $ 1/\sigma_E $, followed by the non-Markovian relaxation of Sec.~\ref{sec:lenk-mz}.

The stationary values are close to thermal ones.
The long-time occupations, $ (7.53, 4.69, 3.43, 2.48, 1.88) $, agree with the canonical values at the same mean energy, $ (7.53, 4.90, 3.36, 2.41, 1.80) $, within $ 4\% $.
At $ N = 25 $ the agreement is $ 9\% $ for $ n_1 $ and better than $ 5\% $ for the other levels: $ (6.61, 6.05, 4.93, 4.04, 3.36) $ versus $ (7.28, 5.80, 4.72, 3.91, 3.29) $.
The deviations do not decrease monotonically with $ N $ in this range.
The deviations are not caused by the energy distribution of the initial state: averaging the smooth energy dependence of the eigenstate expectation values $ \langle a | \hat n_i | a \rangle $ over that distribution reproduces the canonical values to $ 1 $--$ 3\% $ (checked for $ N = 10, 15 $).
They come from the fluctuations of $ \langle a | \hat n_i | a \rangle $ between neighbouring eigenstates, which are correlated with the overlaps $ |\langle a | \psi(0) \rangle|^2 $ of the initial Fock state: a finite-size memory of the initial condition, of the same origin as the plateau of Fig.~\ref{fig:lenk-mz}(c).

For the entropy, the appropriate reference is not the thermal entropy of S.
The global state is pure, so the entropy of S equals that of R, and R contains at most $ N - n_{\mathrm{sys}} + 1 $ states for each value of $ n_{\mathrm{sys}} $.
The Schmidt rank is therefore at most $ \sum_{n_{\mathrm{sys}}} \min(d_S, d_R) $, with $ d_S $ and $ d_R $ the dimensions of the two factors in each block, which bounds the entropy by $ \ln 171 = 5.14 $ for $ N = 20 $ and $ \ln 266 = 5.58 $ for $ N = 25 $.
Neither part is small compared with the other, so the entropy of a pure state stays below the thermal entropy of either part.
The reference is the entropy of S in a canonical-typical pure state, $ |\psi\rangle = \sum_a \sqrt{p_a} \, e^{\ii\varphi_a} |a\rangle $ with canonical weights $ p_a $ and random phases $ \varphi_a $: $ 4.786 \pm 0.010 $ for $ N = 20 $.
The long-time value after the quench is $ 4.585 \pm 0.010 $, $ 4\% $ lower.
The late-time state differs from a canonical-typical state only in its weights $ |\langle a | \psi(0) \rangle|^2 $, so the difference is due to these weights. They differ from the canonical ones both in their energy distribution, which is half as wide [Fig.~\ref{fig:lenk-quench}(c)], and in their eigenstate-to-eigenstate structure.
At $ N = 25 $ the long-time entropy is $ 5.145 \pm 0.006 $, which reproduces $ \bar S_\infty = 5.15 $ of Ref.~\cite{Lenk2025}, while the canonical-typical value is $ 5.354 \pm 0.002 $, again $ 4\% $ higher.
The observed subsystem thus thermalizes, in the sense that its few-body observables and its entanglement entropy reach the values of a thermal pure state of the whole, up to deviations of a few per cent (at most $ 9\% $ for the occupations at $ N = 25 $) that reflect a finite-size memory of the initial state.

\subsection{The whole does not thermalize}

The whole remains far from equilibrium, in three exactly verifiable ways.
First, its state is pure at all times, with zero entropy, while every Gibbs state has an extensive one.
Second, the energy distribution $ p_E = |\langle E | \psi(0) \rangle|^2 $ is a constant of motion; for the Fock initial state at $ N = 20 $ it has width $ \sigma_E = 44 $, compared with $ 90 $ for the canonical state [Fig.~\ref{fig:lenk-quench}(c)], and it keeps this form forever.
Third, the trace distance to any Gibbs state is constant, by Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariants}.
What thermalizes is the reduced description of the observed levels, whose environment, the remaining degrees of freedom of the same closed system, acts as an internal bath.

\subsection{Critique of Lenk et al.}
\label{sec:lenk-critique}

The numerical findings \ref{r:entropy} and \ref{r:nsys} and the near-thermal occupations are reproduced here; at $ N = 25 $ the stationary entropy agrees with $ \bar S_\infty = 5.15 $ to three digits.
The authors' central idea is also supported: the unobserved part of a closed, non-integrable system acts as a reservoir for the observed part, and the subsystem, not the whole, approaches thermal behaviour.
The interpretation, however, needs the following corrections.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.2em]
\item Measurement plays no dynamical role. The partition into S and R is a choice of relevant variables, i.e.\ of the projector $ \PP $, and the reduced dynamics is the same whether or not a measurement is made. ``Measurement-induced bath generation'' is therefore not a separate mechanism: it is the relaxation of a subsystem inside a closed chaotic system. What makes R act as a bath is the dephasing of the memory kernel over $ \propto D $ incommensurate frequencies (Sec.~\ref{sec:mz-method}), which requires non-integrability, as the authors state; the partition alone does not. For $ U = U' = 0 $ the Hamiltonian is quadratic, its single-particle eigenmode occupations are conserved, and the same partition would produce entanglement but relaxation to a GGE.
\item The ``bath'' is not the larger part. R holds most of the particles, but in each block of fixed $ n_{\mathrm{sys}} $ it has at most $ N - n_{\mathrm{sys}} + 1 \le 26 $ states, fewer than S for most blocks. The entanglement entropy is bounded by $ \ln 266 = 5.58 $ at $ N = 25 $ and saturates at the value of a thermal pure state, not at a thermal entropy of S. The claim that the maximum of the entanglement entropy ``represents a thermal state'' is tested in Ref.~\cite{Lenk2025} only indirectly, through \ref{r:spectra} and \ref{r:corr}; the direct test is the comparison with a canonical-typical pure state made above.
\item The two time constants are not two relaxation modes. The relaxation spectrum has no gap [Fig.~\ref{fig:lenk-mz}(d)], and the memory kernel of $ n_{\mathrm{sys}} $ gives no Markov rate. The fast constants of \ref{r:entropy} and \ref{r:nsys} differ by a factor of six ($ 0.26/J $ and $ 0.041/J $); the faster one is of the order of the inverse energy width of the initial state, $ 1/\sigma_E = 0.017/J $ at $ N = 25 $ ($ \sigma_E = 60 $), the time on which the Fock state decays into eigenstates. The bi-exponential form describes the data in the fit window but carries no further physical content.
\item The narrow state is static for a different reason than stated in \ref{r:narrow}. Its $ 74 $ eigenstates span $ 1.7\,J $, so its dynamics is not restricted to a single frequency. It is static because an equal-weight superposition of many eigenstates in a narrow window is already a typical state, whose reduced state is thermal at $ t = 0 $ (canonical typicality~\cite{Goldstein2006,Popescu2006}); accordingly its initial entanglement entropy is already large.
\item Broad energy distributions and correlations between levels are within the scope of the ETH. For any initial state, stationary expectation values are diagonal-ensemble averages, $ \sum_a |\langle a|\psi(0)\rangle|^2 \langle a|\hat O|a\rangle $; the ETH makes them thermal when $ \langle a|\hat O|a\rangle $ varies smoothly on the scale of the energy width, here $ \sigma_E/E \approx 0.1 $. With five levels, the products $ \hat n_i \hat n_j $ are few-body operators to which the ETH applies. The residual deviations found above are finite-size fluctuations of exactly the kind the ETH describes. The ETH indeed does not predict rates, since it leaves the function $ f_O $ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:eth} unspecified, but neither does the bath-generation picture: the time constants of \ref{r:entropy}--\ref{r:nsys} and the temperature of \ref{r:spectra} are numerical results, not predictions. The Mori--Zwanzig kernel is the framework in which rates can be computed.
\item The temperature fit has one free parameter too few. Setting $ \mu = 0 $ is justified in Ref.~\cite{Lenk2025} by the particle number of S not being fixed; but a subsystem exchanging particles with R has the chemical potential of R, which is not zero in general, while the total $ N $ is fixed. At $ T/J \approx 200 $, well above most peak energies $ E_i $, the Bose function is close to $ T/E_i - \frac12 $, and $ \sum_i n_i = N $ then fixes $ T \simeq (N + M/2) / \sum_i E_i^{-1} $: the fitted temperature is largely determined by the particle number and the peak positions. The fitted value nevertheless lies within $ 5\% $ of the canonical temperature of the whole at the quench energy, $ T/J = 207 $ at $ N = 25 $: the single-particle spectra are consistent with the many-body temperature, but the fit with $ \mu $ fixed tests the high-temperature shape $ n_i \propto 1/E_i $ rather than determining $ T $ and $ \mu $ independently. The extraction formula printed in Ref.~\cite{Lenk2025}, $ n_{\mathrm B} = \ii \bar G^K_i/\bar A_i - \frac12 $, is inconsistent with their fluctuation--dissipation relation $ G^K = -\ii A (2 n_{\mathrm B} + 1) $, which gives $ n_{\mathrm B} = (\ii G^K/A - 1)/2 $; this is presumably a misprint.
\item The role of the Hilbert-space dimension is confirmed, with a caveat. Finite-size memory scales as $ D^{-1/2} $ [Fig.~\ref{fig:lenk-mz}(c)], supporting the statement that $ D $, not $ N $, controls the approach to the thermodynamic limit. At fixed $ M = 5 $, however, $ N \to \infty $ is the semiclassical limit, with effective Planck constant $ \propto 1/N $, of a system of five coupled nonlinear modes, whose ergodicity is not guaranteed by the growth of $ D $.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions and Outlook}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Table~\ref{tab:summary} collects the four cases.
In all of them the whole does not thermalize, as required by Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariants}.
A reduced description reaches a steady state when its memory kernel decays.
In a closed system this happens by dephasing, not by loss of information. It requires a bath, external or internal, large enough that its frequencies are dense on the time scale of observation, and free of additional conservation laws that would keep part of the kernel from decaying.
The steady state is a Gibbs state at a given temperature when the bath obeys detailed balance at that temperature.
When the reduced variables are, in addition, weakly coupled to the bath, i.e.\ slow in the sense of Eq.~\eqref{eq:markov-condition}, the approach is Markovian and follows from the bath's two-point function alone.
An external bath that is large, weakly coupled and thermal supplies all of this (Secs.~\ref{sec:bath}, \ref{sec:quantum}).
Inside a closed system, the remaining degrees of freedom can act as the bath (Sec.~\ref{sec:mz} and Appendix~\ref{app:fput}).
In the Bose gas of Lenk \textit{et al.}\ they do: the observed occupations reach values within a few per cent of thermal ones, with a finite-size memory that scales as $ D^{-1/2} $ in equilibrium. But the relaxation spectrum has no gap, so the only slow variable is the energy, and the approach is non-Markovian.

More generally, slow and fast are the two ends of a hierarchy of relaxation rates.
Which variables form the system and which the bath is fixed by the time scale of observation: faster variables have relaxed and act as the bath, slower ones are frozen and act as constants of motion, and the state reached is the constrained equilibrium~\eqref{eq:constrained}, with a multiplier, and possibly a temperature, for each frozen variable.
The line between system and bath is well defined only where the rates have a gap; in the Bose gas they have none.

The following calculations and experiments would extend these results.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item Determine the conditions under which an internal bath is weakly coupled. Candidates are few observed levels tunnel-coupled to a large, dense set of unobserved levels, for which $ K(0)\tau_c^2 $ can be tuned, and larger $ M $ at fixed filling. Krylov or dynamical-typicality methods reach Hilbert-space dimensions of $ 10^8 $ and would show whether a Markovian window opens with system size.
\item Compute the Nakajima--Zwanzig kernel for the Fock populations of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lenk-H}. It would connect the fast decay of the initial state (width $ \sigma_E $) and the slow redistribution to explicit rates, and identify the non-Markovian corrections responsible for the revivals.
\item Test the thermality of reduced states by the generalized fluctuation--dissipation relation~\eqref{eq:beta-eff}, i.e.\ by measuring noise and response of the same observable, in systems with controllable coupling to a bath (superconducting circuits, trapped-ion phonon modes, nanomechanical resonators), from weak coupling, where $ \beta_{\mathrm{eff}} $ should be constant, to strong coupling, where the Hamiltonian of mean force takes over.
\item In ultracold gases in multi-level traps or coupled condensates~\cite{Posazhennikova2016}, compare the relaxation of level occupations with and without an integrability-breaking perturbation~\cite{Kinoshita2006,Langen2015}, and measure the finite-size residual fluctuations predicted to scale as $ D^{-1/2} $.
\item Measure the temperature dependence of Debye--Waller factors or interferometric visibilities together with the lattice entropy, to test the relation between single-probe decoherence and bulk thermodynamics (Sec.~\ref{sec:debye}).

\section{Integrable and Non-Integrable Dynamics}
\label{app:integrability}

Whether a system has conserved quantities beyond the energy (Sec.~\ref{sec:defs}) is the distinction between integrable and non-integrable systems.

\paragraph{Classical systems.}
A Hamiltonian system with $ f $ degrees of freedom is (Liouville) integrable if it has $ f $ independent constants of motion $ I_1, \dots, I_f $ in involution, $ \{ I_k , I_l \} = 0 $.
Each trajectory then lies on an $ f $-dimensional torus labelled by the values of the $ I_k $. In the action--angle variables of that torus the actions are constant and the angles advance uniformly, so the motion is quasi-periodic.
A trajectory explores only its torus, not the energy shell.
A system with fewer than $ f $ such constants is non-integrable.
Non-integrability is the generic case: an arbitrary interaction destroys the extra constants of motion, and parts of the energy shell become chaotic, with nearby trajectories separating exponentially in time.
A weak perturbation of an integrable system is special: by the Kolmogorov--Arnold--Moser (KAM) theorem, most tori then survive in deformed form, and chaos is confined to thin layers between them~\cite{Berman2005}.

\paragraph{Quantum systems.}
Counting conserved operators does not work in quantum mechanics, because every Hamiltonian has as many commuting constants of motion as its Hilbert-space dimension: the projectors $ |a\rangle\langle a| $ onto its eigenstates.
The accepted working definition~\cite{Caux2011} counts only conserved quantities with physical structure, i.e.\ sums of few-body (or local) operators.
A many-body system is integrable if it has a number of independent such conserved quantities that grows with the number of degrees of freedom; it is non-integrable if only a few exist, typically the energy, the particle number and the momentum.
The standard example of an integrable system is a quadratic Hamiltonian, whose single-particle eigenmode occupations are all conserved; interacting models solvable by the Bethe ansatz are others.
An operational test is the statistics of the energy levels.
Levels of integrable systems are uncorrelated (Poisson statistics), because the many conserved quantities split the spectrum into independent sectors.
Levels of non-integrable systems repel each other and follow random-matrix (Wigner--Dyson) statistics~\cite{BerryTabor1977,Bohigas1984}.
A convenient measure is the mean ratio $ \langle r \rangle $ of consecutive level spacings~\cite{Oganesyan2007,Atas2013}: $ \langle r\rangle \simeq 0.386 $ for Poisson statistics and $ 0.531 $ for the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble of real symmetric random matrices.
Non-integrability is the quantum counterpart of chaos, and it is the condition under which few-body observables of a closed system are expected to thermalize~\cite{DAlessio2016}.

\section{The Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis}
\label{app:eth}

\paragraph{The question.}
A closed quantum system prepared in a pure state $ |\psi(0)\rangle = \sum_a c_a |a\rangle $, with energy eigenstates $ |a\rangle $, evolves as
\begin{equation}
  \langle \hat O \rangle(t) = \sum_{a,b} c_a^* c_b \, e^{\ii(E_a - E_b)t} \, O_{ab} ,
  \qquad
  O_{ab} = \langle a | \hat O | b \rangle .
  \label{eq:eth-evolution}
\end{equation}
For non-degenerate energies the off-diagonal terms dephase, and the long-time average is the diagonal-ensemble average $ \bar O = \sum_a |c_a|^2 O_{aa} $.
It depends on all $ D $ weights $ |c_a|^2 $, which are constants of motion.
Thermalization requires instead that $ \bar O $ depend only on the energy.
The eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH) explains how, through a property of the matrix elements $ O_{ab} $.
Its precursor is von Neumann's quantum ergodic theorem~\cite{vonNeumann1929,Goldstein2010}.
It was formulated by Deutsch~\cite{Deutsch1991}, for an integrable Hamiltonian perturbed by a random matrix, and by Srednicki~\cite{Srednicki1994}, for chaotic systems whose eigenfunctions behave as random superpositions of plane waves (Berry's conjecture~\cite{Berry1977}). Srednicki extended it to off-diagonal elements~\cite{Srednicki1999}. It was verified numerically for interacting lattice bosons by Rigol, Dunjko and Olshanii~\cite{Rigol2008}. Comprehensive reviews are Refs.~\cite{DAlessio2016,Deutsch2018}.

\paragraph{Premises.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(P\arabic*), leftmargin=3.0em]
\item The Hamiltonian is non-integrable (Appendix~\ref{app:integrability}), with Wigner--Dyson level statistics.
\item The observable is few-body or local. The hypothesis is not meant for arbitrary operators, such as the projectors $ |a\rangle\langle a| $, which are conserved.
\item The eigenstates lie in the bulk of the spectrum, where the density of states $ e^{S(E)} $ is large. $ S(E) $ is the thermodynamic entropy of the whole at energy $ E $.
\item The statements are asymptotic in system size; corrections are controlled by $ e^{-S/2} $.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Statement.}
In the form given by Srednicki~\cite{Srednicki1999},
\begin{equation}
  O_{ab} = O(\bar E) \, \delta_{ab} + e^{-S(\bar E)/2} \, f_O(\bar E, \omega) \, R_{ab} ,
  \qquad
  \bar E = \tfrac12 (E_a + E_b) , \quad \omega = E_b - E_a .
  \label{eq:eth}
\end{equation}
Here $ O(E) $ and $ f_O(E,\omega) $ are smooth functions of their arguments, and $ R_{ab} $ are pseudo-random numbers with zero mean and unit variance.
$ O(E) $ equals the microcanonical average of $ \hat O $ at energy $ E $.
Hermiticity of $ \hat O $ requires $ |f_O(E,-\omega)| = |f_O(E,\omega)| $.
The diagonal elements are thus a smooth function of energy, up to fluctuations of relative size $ e^{-S/2} $ between neighbouring eigenstates.
The off-diagonal elements are exponentially small, with a smooth frequency profile $ f_O $.
The weak form of the hypothesis requires this for most eigenstates; the strong form requires it for all eigenstates in the bulk of the spectrum~\cite{Biroli2010,Kim2014}.

\paragraph{Consequences.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(E\arabic*), leftmargin=3.0em]
\item Stationary values. For an initial state with mean energy $ E $ and energy width $ \delta E $, Eq.~\eqref{eq:eth} gives $ \bar O = O(E) + \tfrac12 O''(E)\,\delta E^2 + O(e^{-S/2}) $. For local Hamiltonians $ \delta E $ grows only as the square root of the system size, while $ O(E) $ varies on extensive energy scales, so $ \bar O $ approaches the microcanonical value. The initial state is forgotten except for its energy, for broad as well as narrow energy distributions in this sense. In an integrable system $ O_{aa} $ depends on all conserved quantities, not on the energy alone, and $ \bar O $ is a GGE average.
\item Small temporal fluctuations. For non-degenerate energy gaps, the time-averaged squared deviation from $ \bar O $ is
\begin{equation*}
  \overline{\big( \langle\hat O\rangle(t) - \bar O \big)^2}
  = \sum_{a \neq b} |c_a|^2 |c_b|^2 |O_{ab}|^2
  \le \max_{a\neq b} |O_{ab}|^2 \sum_a |c_a|^4 ,
\end{equation*}
with $ \max |O_{ab}|^2 \propto e^{-S} $ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:eth}. Once dephased, the expectation value stays close to $ \bar O $ for almost all times~\cite{Reimann2008}.
\item Thermal eigenstates. A single eigenstate reproduces the thermal values of all few-body observables. The reduced density matrix of a subsystem that is small compared with the rest is thermal in each eigenstate~\cite{Garrison2018}. Thermalization is thus encoded in the eigenstates; the dynamics only dephases the initial superposition, the mechanism of Sec.~\ref{sec:mz-method}.
\item Detailed balance within an eigenstate. In the eigenstate $ |a\rangle $, the correlation functions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bath-corr} become $ C^>(\omega) = 2\pi \sum_b |O_{ab}|^2 \delta(\omega - E_b + E_a) = 2\pi |f_O(E_a + \omega/2, \omega)|^2 \, e^{S(E_a + \omega) - S(E_a + \omega/2)} $, and similarly for $ C^< $ with $ \omega \to -\omega $. Expanding the entropies to first order in $ \omega $ gives $ C^>(\omega)/C^<(\omega) = e^{\beta\omega} $ with $ \beta = \partial S/\partial E $. Each eigenstate thus satisfies the KMS condition at the microcanonical temperature, and the fluctuation--dissipation theorem holds for few-body observables of a closed system~\cite{DAlessio2016}. The temperature enters through the density of states of the whole.
\item Finite-size corrections. The eigenstate-to-eigenstate fluctuations of $ O_{aa} $, of order $ e^{-S/2} $, are the finite-size memory of the initial state. Their scaling has been verified numerically in lattice models~\cite{Beugeling2014,Kim2014}. In the Bose gas of Sec.~\ref{sec:mz}, $ e^{S} $ is proportional to $ D $, and the $ D^{-1/2} $ plateau of Fig.~\ref{fig:lenk-mz}(c) is this prediction.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limits.}
The ETH is a hypothesis, supported by extensive numerical evidence and proved only for random-matrix models.
It fails for integrable systems, where the stationary state is a GGE; for many-body localized systems, whose eigenstates are not thermal~\cite{Nandkishore2015}; and for rare non-thermal eigenstates (quantum many-body scars) in otherwise chaotic systems~\cite{Turner2018}.
It leaves the function $ f_O(E,\omega) $ unspecified, and with it the relaxation times: the ETH predicts where a system relaxes to, not how fast.
Canonical typicality~\cite{Goldstein2006,Popescu2006} is a complementary, kinematic statement: most pure states in an energy shell have thermal reduced states. The ETH states that every eigenstate is such a state, which guarantees relaxation from any initial state with a sufficiently narrow energy distribution, not only from typical ones.

\section{Anharmonic Chain: The FPUT Problem}
\label{app:fput}

The chain of Sec.~\ref{sec:chain-numerics} is the one studied numerically by Fermi, Pasta, Ulam and Tsingou (FPUT)~\cite{FPU1955}, who expected an anharmonic coupling of the modes to produce equipartition.
Adding a quartic term to the spring potential, $ V(r) = \kappa r^2/2 + \beta r^4/4 $ with $ r = x_{j+1} - x_j $, couples the normal modes.
This is the $ \beta $-FPUT chain~\cite{FPU1955,Berman2005}, named after the coefficient $ \beta $ of the quartic term (a cubic term, with coefficient $ \alpha $, defines the $ \alpha $-FPUT chain).
The chain remains closed, conserves energy, and still obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariants}, but it is no longer integrable.
With $ \beta = \kappa/a^2 $ and the same initial conditions as in Fig.~\ref{fig:chain}, $ n_{\mathrm{eff}} $ reaches the equipartition value for $ \omega_0 t \gtrsim 3\times 10^3 $, $ c_1 $ decays to zero, and the long-time mode spectrum is flat (Fig.~\ref{fig:fput}).
The distribution of mode energies, a reduced description, has thermalized: each mode exchanges energy with all others, which act as its bath.
This is the classical counterpart of the internal bath analyzed in Sec.~\ref{sec:mz}.
The obstacle to thermalization in Sec.~\ref{sec:chain} is therefore integrability, not the closedness of the chain.
Near integrability the approach can be very slow, since by the KAM theorem (Appendix~\ref{app:integrability}) many invariant tori survive a weak nonlinearity~\cite{Berman2005}.

\section{Numerical Methods}
\label{app:numerics}

The Python scripts in \texttt{code/} generate all figures (\texttt{make figs}).

\paragraph{Harmonic and FPUT chains (Figs.~\ref{fig:chain}, \ref{fig:fput}).}
Units $ m = \kappa = a = 1 $. The equations of motion are integrated with the fourth-order symplectic scheme of Yoshida~\cite{Yoshida1990}, $ \delta t = 0.05 $, up to $ \omega_0 t = 2\times 10^4 $. A symplectic scheme preserves phase-space volume and keeps the energy error bounded; the relative energy error is below $ 10^{-5} $. Mode energies are projections onto the sine modes of Eq.~\eqref{eq:chain-modes}.

\paragraph{System coupled to a bath (Fig.~\ref{fig:bath}).}
Units $ \omega_0 = T = 1 $. The bath frequencies are $ \omega_k = (k - \tfrac12)\,\delta\omega $ with $ \delta\omega = \omega_c/N_B $, and $ c_k^2 = (2/\pi)\,\eta\,\omega_k^2\,\delta\omega $ reproduces the Ohmic density~\eqref{eq:Jclass}. The $ (N_B + 2) \times (N_B + 2) $ stiffness matrix of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:HS}--\eqref{eq:HB} is diagonalized, and the exact solution~\eqref{eq:chain-solution} is evaluated for 4000 realizations: random phases of the system modes, and bath coordinates drawn from the Gibbs state around the displaced equilibrium $ q_k = c_k x_1(0)/\omega_k^2 $.

\paragraph{Bose gas (Figs.~\ref{fig:lenk-mz}, \ref{fig:lenk-quench}).}
The Hamiltonian is built in the Fock basis from Eq.~\eqref{eq:lenk-K}, verified against the term-by-term definition~\eqref{eq:lenk-H} for small $ N $, and diagonalized with LAPACK (80~s for $ D = 10626 $). The Kubo relaxation function is
\begin{equation}
  C(t) = \sum_{a,b} \frac{p_a - p_b}{\beta (E_b - E_a)} \, |A_{ab}|^2 \cos\big[(E_b - E_a) t\big] - \langle A \rangle^2 ,
  \label{eq:kubo-spectral}
\end{equation}
with $ p_a \propto e^{-\beta E_a} $, $ A_{ab} = \langle a | \hat n_{\mathrm{sys}} | b \rangle $, and the factor replaced by $ p_a $ for $ a = b $. The weights are binned on a frequency grid of spacing $ 0.005\,J $ and Fourier transformed. The Mazur term $ \sprod{A}{H}^2/\sprod{H}{H} $ is subtracted, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:volterra} is solved with the trapezoidal rule on the resulting time grid ($ \delta t \approx 2\times 10^{-3}/J $). The quench is propagated exactly in the eigenbasis at 250 logarithmically spaced times. The relaxation spectrum uses the same binned Fourier transform for all pairs of the eight candidate variables ($ N = 10, 15 $). The $ N = 25 $ quench observables ($ D = 23\,751 $, diagonalization about 15~min) are computed by \texttt{code/lenk\_check.py}; the late-time entropy is averaged over 41 times in $ 20 \le Jt \le 100 $. Reduced density matrices of levels 3--5 are assembled block by block in $ n_{\mathrm{sys}} $. The canonical-typical reference is averaged over 8 sets of random phases.
